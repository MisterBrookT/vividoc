Intersection of Circles

Exploring the planar geometry and mathematics of overlapping circles.

1. Fundamentals

In Euclidean geometry, the intersection of two circles result in either 0, 1, or 2 points. When we extend this to multiple circles, we look for the common intersection area (the Venn diagram approach) or the specific points where all circumferences meet.

Three key cases for two circles with radii $r_1, r_2$ and distance between centers $d$:

  • Separate: $d > r_1 + r_2$ (No intersection)
  • Tangent: $d = r_1 + r_2$ (1 intersection point)
  • Overlapping: $|r_1 - r_2| < d < r_1 + r_2$ (2 intersection points)

3. The Algebra of trilateration

Finding the intersection of circles is the basis for Trilateration, the technology used in GPS. Given two circles $(x-x_1)^2 + (y-y_1)^2 = r_1^2$ and $(x-x_2)^2 + (y-y_2)^2 = r_2^2$:

1. Subtract the equations to find the Radical Line (the line passing through intersection points).
2. Solve for 'a' (distance from center 1 to the radical line): a = (r1² - r2² + d²) / (2d)
3. Solve for 'h' (height of intersection from radical line): h = sqrt(r1² - a²)
4. Coordinate P = C1 + a/d * (C2 - C1) ± h/d * (y2-y1, -(x2-x1))

4. Real-World Applications

  • GNSS/GPS: Determining a 3D position using the intersection of spheres (estimated as circles on a 2D map).
  • Wireless Networking: Determining the coverage area where multiple Wi-Fi or Cellular signals overlap.
  • Structural Engineering: Calculating the area of reinforced zones in overlapping support column footprints.
