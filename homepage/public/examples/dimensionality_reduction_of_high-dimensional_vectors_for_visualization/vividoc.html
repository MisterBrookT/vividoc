Dimensionality reduction of high-dimensional vectors for visualization

In modern machine learning, data is often represented as vectors in spaces with hundreds or even thousands of dimensions. While we can easily visualize a vector in 2D or 3D, our spatial intuition fails completely when faced with a 768-dimensional embedding from a language model. This cognitive barrier is not just a matter of perspective; it is a fundamental mathematical challenge known as the Curse of Dimensionality.

One of the most counter-intuitive phenomena in high-dimensional spaces is the way distances behave. As the number of dimensions $d$ increases, the distance between any two random points tends to converge. If we consider a hypercube where points are distributed uniformly, the ratio between the distance of the farthest point and the nearest point to the origin approaches zero as $d \to \infty$. Mathematically, for a distance metric $dist$, this is expressed as:

$\lim_{d \to \infty} \frac{dist_{max} - dist_{min}}{dist_{min}} = 0$

This "thinning" of contrast makes it incredibly difficult to identify clusters or patterns, as every point appears roughly equidistant from every other point. To make sense of this data, we must employ dimensionality reduction—the process of transforming a dataset from a high-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^D$ into a lower-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^k$ (where $k$ is usually 2 or 3 for visualization) while retaining the meaningful structure of the original data.

Effective projection techniques aim to solve a core optimization problem: how do we discard the majority of the information while preserving specific topological or statistical properties? Some methods focus on preserving the global variance of the data (PCA), while others prioritize maintaining local neighborhoods (t-SNE or UMAP), ensuring that points that were "close" in the high-dimensional space remain "close" on our 2D screen.

While random projections offer a glimpse into high-dimensional data, they often obscure the underlying structure by choosing arbitrary axes. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) provides a more rigorous approach by identifying a new set of orthogonal axes—the Principal Components—ranked by the amount of variance they capture. Instead of looking at the data from a random angle, PCA mathematically rotates the coordinate system to find the "widest" spans of the data distribution.

The first principal component $u_1$ is defined as the direction that maximizes the variance of the projected data. Once $u_1$ is found, the second component $u_2$ is chosen to be orthogonal to the first while maximizing the remaining variance. Mathematically, for a centered data matrix $X$, this involves finding the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix $\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1}X^T X$. The optimization objective for the first component can be expressed as:

$\max_{u: \|u\|=1} u^T \Sigma u$

By selecting the top $k$ eigenvectors (where $k=2$ or $3$ for visualization), we project the data into a lower-dimensional subspace that retains the maximum possible global variance. This ensures that the most prominent features—the "broad strokes" of the dataset—are preserved, even if the fine-grained local relationships are somewhat compressed.

This linear transformation is essentially a rigid rotation and scaling. Consequently, PCA is excellent for uncovering the global geometry and identifying linear correlations between dimensions. However, because it relies on global variance, it can struggle to visualize complex, non-linear manifolds where points might be "close" along a curved path but "far" in terms of Euclidean distance. In such cases, the top two components might overlap distinct clusters that are only separable through non-linear lenses.

While PCA projects data onto a linear subspace, many real-world datasets—such as image embeddings or single-cell RNA sequences—reside on complex, curved non-linear manifolds. To visualize these, we turn to t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE). Unlike PCA, which prioritizes the preservation of large distances (global variance), t-SNE focuses on local structure: ensuring that points belonging to the same cluster in high-dimensional space remain neighbors in the 2D projection.

The algorithm operates in two stages. First, it converts Euclidean distances between high-dimensional points into conditional probabilities $p_{j|i}$ that represent similarities. For a point $x_i$, the probability that it would pick $x_j$ as its neighbor is modeled by a Gaussian distribution centered at $x_i$:

$p_{j|i} = \frac{\exp(-\|x_i - x_j\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(-\|x_i - x_k\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}$

In the low-dimensional space, t-SNE uses a Student’s t-distribution with one degree of freedom to model similarities $q_{ij}$. This "heavy-tailed" distribution is a clever solution to the Crowding Problem: in low dimensions, there is simply not enough "room" to preserve all distances accurately. The t-distribution allows moderately distant points in high-D to be pushed much further apart in 2D, preventing clusters from collapsing into a single dense blob.

The final layout is reached through an iterative optimization process, typically using gradient descent to minimize the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the high-dimensional distribution $P$ and the low-dimensional distribution $Q$. A critical hyperparameter in this process is perplexity, which can be thought of as a smooth measure of the effective number of neighbors. Adjusting perplexity allows the user to balance the algorithm's attention between microscopic local details and the macroscopic global cluster structure.
