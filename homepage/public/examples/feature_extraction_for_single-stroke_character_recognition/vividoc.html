Feature extraction for single-stroke character recognition

When a user draws a character on a digital interface, the device captures a raw sequence of points $P = \{p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n\}$, where each point $p_i = (x_i, y_i)$. However, this raw data is inherently inconsistent. Because sensors sample data at constant time intervals, the spatial density of points depends entirely on the writing speed: a slow, deliberate stroke creates a dense cluster of points, while a rapid flick results in sparse, widely spaced points.

To extract meaningful geometric features, we must decouple the character's shape from the dynamics of the motion. This is achieved through resampling—the process of transforming the raw stroke into a new sequence of $N$ points that are equidistant along the path. By standardizing the number of points (typically $N=32$ or $N=64$), we ensure that the feature vector has a fixed dimension regardless of the original stroke's length or speed.

The resampling process begins by calculating the total path length $L$ of the stroke. Given the Euclidean distance $d(p_i, p_{i+1})$ between consecutive points, the total length is defined as:

$$L = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} d(p_i, p_{i+1})$$

We then determine the target inter-point interval, denoted as $I = \frac{L}{N-1}$. The algorithm iterates through the original points, greedily adding new points at every distance $I$ along the segments of the polyline. If the distance to the next raw point is less than $I$, we move to the next segment; if it exceeds $I$, we use linear interpolation to find the exact coordinates of the new point. This produces a normalized representation where the spatial relationship between points is uniform, effectively "smoothing" the input and highlighting the structural topology of the character.

After resampling the stroke to a uniform spatial density, the character remains sensitive to global transformations. A user might draw the letter "A" in the top-left corner of a tablet or the bottom-right; they might draw it as a tiny glyph or a large, sweeping gesture. If the raw coordinates were passed directly to a classifier, the system would treat these variations as entirely different patterns. To achieve translation and scale invariance, we must map the stroke into a standardized reference frame, typically a unit square centered at the origin $(0,0)$.

The first step is translation normalization. We find the stroke's centroid $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ by averaging the coordinates of the resampled points: $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{N} \sum x_i$ and $\bar{y} = \frac{1}{N} \sum y_i$. Every point $p_i$ is then shifted by subtracting this centroid: $p'_i = (x_i - \bar{x}, y_i - \bar{y})$. This centering process ensures that the position of the character on the screen no longer affects its numerical representation.

Next, we address scale normalization. Even when centered, a large "S" and a small "S" have different coordinate ranges. We define a bounding box for the stroke, determined by its minimum and maximum coordinates. Let $w$ and $h$ be the width and height of this box. To fit the character into a unit square of size $S$, we apply a scaling factor: $$x''_i = x'_i \cdot \frac{S}{\max(w, h)}, \quad y''_i = y'_i \cdot \frac{S}{\max(w, h)}$$ By using the maximum dimension (aspect-ratio preservation), we avoid distorting the character’s shape—for instance, preventing a tall, thin "1" from being stretched into a square. This resulting canonical form ensures that the recognizer focuses strictly on the relative geometry and structural topology of the stroke, rather than where or how large it was drawn.

With the stroke now resampled and normalized, we possess a sequence of $N$ coordinates in a canonical space. While these points define the shape, passing absolute coordinates directly into a classifier can still be sensitive to local distortions. To create a more robust descriptor, we shift our focus from positional data to directional data. By analyzing the orientation of the segments connecting our resampled points, we can characterize the "flow" or curvature of the stroke, which is often more indicative of a user’s intent than the exact placement of a point.

For every pair of consecutive resampled points $p_i = (x_i, y_i)$ and $p_{i+1} = (x_{i+1}, y_{i+1})$, we treat the segment between them as a vector. We calculate the angle $\theta_i$ that this segment makes with the positive x-axis using the arctangent function:

$$\theta_i = \operatorname{atan2}(y_{i+1} - y_i, x_{i+1} - x_i)$$

However, raw angles present a mathematical challenge: circularity. An angle of $0$ radians is numerically far from $2\pi$, yet they represent the exact same direction. To map these angles into a continuous linear space suitable for machine learning, we decompose each angle into its trigonometric components: $\cos(\theta_i)$ and $\sin(\theta_i)$. This transformation yields a feature vector of length $2(N-1)$.

The resulting vector $V = [\cos\theta_1, \sin\theta_1, \cos\theta_2, \sin\theta_2, \dots]$ provides a powerful, fixed-length representation of the stroke's geometry. Because trigonometric functions are bounded between $[-1, 1]$, the features are inherently normalized. This directional feature vector is the engine behind many classic recognizers, such as the $1 Unistroke Recognizer. It effectively captures the structural "signature" of a gesture—making it easy to distinguish between the sharp vertex of a "V" and the smooth, continuous rotation of an "O"—while remaining resilient to the slight wobbles or "noise" inherent in human handwriting.
