Waveform properties and additive synthesis

If we want to understand complexity, we must first master the simple. In the world of acoustics, the sine wave is the purest possible sound—a single, smooth oscillation containing no additional harmonics or overtones. To understand how this wave functions, we must look at its two defining characteristics: amplitude and frequency.

Amplitude refers to the maximum displacement of the wave from its equilibrium (the center line). Visually, this is the "height" of the wave's peaks and the "depth" of its troughs. In auditory terms, amplitude is the primary determinant of volume or intensity. The greater the energy driving the oscillation, the higher the amplitude, and the louder the sound we perceive. Mathematically, if we describe a simple wave as a function of time ($t$), the amplitude is the coefficient $A$ in the expression:

$y(t) = A \sin(2\pi f t)$

While amplitude dictates volume, frequency dictates pitch. Frequency is defined as the number of complete cycles the wave performs in one second, measured in Hertz (Hz). There is an inverse relationship between frequency and wavelength ($\lambda$): as the frequency increases, the cycles become more compressed, resulting in a shorter wavelength. A wave vibrating at $440 \text{ Hz}$ (the standard musical note A4) completes 440 cycles every second. If we double that frequency to $880 \text{ Hz}$, the wavelength is halved, and we perceive a pitch exactly one octave higher.

When you observe these properties on an oscilloscope, the relationship is intuitive. Increasing the amplitude stretches the wave vertically, making it taller and louder. Increasing the frequency squeezes the wave horizontally, making it denser and higher in pitch. These two parameters are the fundamental "coordinates" used to map any sound in the additive synthesis process.

Warning: Pure tones can be sharp; start with low volume.

In nature, a perfectly isolated sine wave is a rarity. Most sounds we encounter—from the vibrating string of a cello to the human voice—are composite signals. While the sine wave represents a single, pure frequency, musical timbre is born from the interplay of multiple frequencies vibrating simultaneously. This brings us to the core principle of additive synthesis: the ability to construct any complex periodic waveform by summing together a series of simple sine waves.

The foundation of this architecture is the fundamental frequency ($f$), also known as the first harmonic. This is the lowest frequency in the stack and typically determines the perceived pitch of the sound. When we add additional sine waves at integer multiples of this frequency—such as $2f$, $3f$, $4f$, and so on—we are building a harmonic series. These additional layers are called harmonics or overtones. For example, if our fundamental is $100 \text{ Hz}$, the second harmonic vibrates at $200 \text{ Hz}$, the third at $300 \text{ Hz}$, and the nth harmonic follows the simple linear relationship:

$f_n = n \times f_1$

When these waves combine, they undergo constructive and destructive interference. This means that at any given moment in time ($t$), the resulting displacement of the air (or the voltage in a wire) is simply the sum of all individual waves. If we denote the amplitude of each harmonic as $A_n$, the total complex wave $y(t)$ can be expressed as:

$y(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} A_n \sin(2\pi \cdot n f t)$

Visually, the smooth, circular symmetry of the sine wave begins to distort as harmonics are introduced. Adding a second harmonic ($2f$) might sharpen the peaks or create a "double-hump" pattern within a single period. This transformation explains why a trumpet and a flute can play the exact same pitch ($f$) yet sound entirely different; they possess different recipes of harmonic amplitudes ($A_n$), creating unique spectral fingerprints that our brains interpret as distinct textures or timbres.

If we continue the process of stacking harmonics indefinitely, we encounter a remarkable mathematical phenomenon: the emergence of geometric shapes. While the previous section demonstrated how a single harmonic can distort a sine wave, additive synthesis allows us to "sculpt" sound into specific, iconic waveforms—the square and sawtooth waves—which serve as the raw materials for electronic music. These shapes are not merely visual curiosities; they represent specific harmonic "recipes" that the human ear perceives as distinct textures.

The square wave is characterized by a "hollow," woody timbre. It is constructed by summing only the odd harmonics ($f_1, 3f_1, 5f_1, \dots$) with amplitudes that decrease in proportion to the harmonic number. To create a perfect square wave, the amplitude of the $n$th harmonic must be $\frac{1}{n}$. As we add more odd partials, the rounded peaks of the sine waves begin to flatten and the slopes become vertical. Mathematically, the series for a square wave is expressed as:

$y_{\text{square}}(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \frac{1}{2k-1} \sin(2\pi(2k-1)ft)$

In contrast, the sawtooth wave possesses a bright, buzzy, and "brassy" quality because it contains every harmonic in the series—both even and odd. Like the square wave, the amplitudes of these harmonics decrease at a rate of $\frac{1}{n}$, but the inclusion of the even-numbered partials ($2f, 4f, 6f, \dots$) prevents the waveform from becoming symmetrical across the horizontal axis. Instead, the cumulative interference creates a sharp "ramp" followed by a sudden vertical drop. The formula for this dense spectral stack is:

$y_{\text{sawtooth}}(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \frac{1}{n} \sin(2\pi nft)$

This process highlights the Fourier Theorem: the principle that any periodic waveform can be broken down into a specific set of sine waves. When you listen to a sawtooth wave, your brain isn't just hearing one sound; it is processing a vast, shimmering ladder of frequencies vibrating in perfect mathematical alignment. By controlling the number of harmonics ($N$) in our synthesis, we can transition from the warm simplicity of a pure tone to the aggressive, harmonically rich complexity of a full-spectrum electronic lead.
