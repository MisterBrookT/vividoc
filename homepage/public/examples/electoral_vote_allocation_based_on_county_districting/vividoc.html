Electoral vote allocation based on county districting

To understand how county-level districting impacts the Electoral College, we must first examine the dominant mechanism used by 48 states and the District of Columbia: the Winner-Take-All system. Under this model, the presidential candidate who receives a plurality of the popular vote within a state boundary—even by a margin of a single vote—is awarded 100% of that state's electoral votes.

Mathematically, this creates a "step function" or a cliff effect, where the relationship between the popular vote share ($V$) and the electoral vote allocation ($E$) is non-linear. If $V_a$ represents the votes for Candidate A and $V_b$ represents the votes for Candidate B in a state with $N$ electoral votes, the allocation follows a simple conditional logic:

If $V_a > V_b$, then $E_a = N$ and $E_b = 0$.

Contrast this with Proportional Representation, a theoretical alternative where electoral votes are distributed to reflect the ratio of the popular vote. In a proportional system, if a candidate wins $51\%$ of the popular vote in a state with $20$ electoral votes, they would receive roughly $10$ or $11$ votes, rather than the full $20$. We can express the proportional allocation ($E_p$) as:

$E_p \approx N \times \left( \frac{V_{candidate}}{V_{total}} \right)$

The Winner-Take-All system magnifies the impact of "swing states," where small shifts in the popular vote—often concentrated in a few key counties—can result in an aggregate shift of dozens of electoral votes. This creates a high-stakes environment where millions of "surplus" votes for the winning candidate, or "lost" votes for the runner-up, do not contribute to the final tally, effectively disenfranchising the minority preference at the state level before the national count even begins.

While most states treat their electoral delegation as a monolithic block, Maine and Nebraska employ the Congressional District Method. This hybrid model avoids the "all-or-nothing" cliff effect by fragmenting the state's total electoral allocation $N$ into two distinct components: the Statewide Bonus ($S$) and the District Mandates ($D$). In this system, $S = 2$ (representing the state's two Senators) and $D$ equals the number of Congressional districts (representing the House delegation), such that $N = S + D$.

The logic of allocation follows a dual-track process. First, the winner of the statewide popular vote is awarded the $S$ bonus. Second, the winner of the plurality in each individual congressional district is awarded $1$ electoral vote for that specific district. Mathematically, for a candidate $i$, the total electoral votes $E_i$ can be expressed as the sum of a statewide Kronecker delta function and the sum of district-level outcomes:

$E_i = 2 \cdot \delta(V_{i, state} > V_{j, state}) + \sum_{k=1}^{D} \delta(V_{i, k} > V_{j, k})$

where $\delta = 1$ if the condition is met and $0$ otherwise. This structure introduces geographic sensitivity into the equation. A candidate can lose the statewide popular vote but still "pierce" the state's delegation by winning a single, culturally or demographically distinct district—often referred to as a "blue dot" in a red state or a "red dot" in a blue state.

This method significantly reduces the volume of wasted votes compared to the Winner-Take-All system. However, it also introduces the risk of gerrymandering into presidential outcomes. Because electoral votes are tied to the boundaries of congressional districts, the way those lines are drawn can predetermine the allocation of electoral votes, potentially decoupling the state's electoral output from its aggregate popular will even more than the standard model.

While the Congressional District Method suggests a more granular representation of the popular will, its structural integrity is entirely dependent on the geometry of the districts. This vulnerability is exploited through Gerrymandering: the intentional manipulation of district boundaries to favor a specific political outcome. Unlike the Winner-Take-All system, which is vulnerable to aggregate shifts, a district-based electoral allocation is vulnerable to the spatial distribution of voters, regardless of the total population's preference.

Two primary tactical mechanisms define this manipulation: Packing and Cracking. In a Packing strategy, the map-maker concentrates as many of the opposition's voters as possible into a single district. While the opposition wins that district by an overwhelming margin, their "surplus" votes are effectively neutralized, preventing them from influencing surrounding contests. Conversely, Cracking involves diffusing the opposition's remaining supporters across multiple districts so that they consistently fall below the $50\% + 1$ threshold required to win a seat. Mathematically, this maximizes the Efficiency Gap ($EG$), defined as the difference between the wasted votes of two parties, divided by the total number of votes cast:

$EG = \frac{W_a - W_b}{V_{total}}$

where $W_a$ represents "wasted" votes (those cast for a losing candidate or surplus votes for a winner). When electoral votes are tied to these districts, gerrymandering transitions from a local legislative concern to a national executive determinant. If a state with $10$ electoral votes is drawn to "crack" a 45% minority into zero districts, that minority loses all potential district-level electoral votes ($D$), leaving them only with the statistical impossibility of winning the statewide bonus ($S$).

This creates a paradoxical "minority squeeze." A candidate can theoretically win the majority of a state’s geographic districts—and thus a majority of its electoral votes—while representing a clear minority of the state's actual citizens. By controlling the spatial logic of the vote, parties can engineer a "safe" electoral floor that resists even significant swings in the popular mood, effectively decoupling the state’s contribution to the Electoral College from its internal democratic aggregate.
