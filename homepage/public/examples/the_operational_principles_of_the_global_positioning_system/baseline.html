How GPS Works

A deep dive into trilateration, timing, and orbital mechanics.

1. The System Architecture

The Global Positioning System (GPS) is composed of three distinct segments working in harmony:

  • Space Segment: A constellation of at least 24 satellites orbiting Earth twice a day. They are arranged so that at least 4 satellites are visible from any point on Earth.
  • Control Segment: Ground stations around the world (managed by the U.S. Space Force) that track satellites, update their orbital data (ephemeris), and synchronize their onboard clocks.
  • User Segment: Your smartphone, car navigation, or watch which receives signals but does not transmit anything back to the satellites.

2. Geometric Trilateration

GPS does not use "triangulation" (which involves angles). Instead, it uses trilateration. By knowing the distance to multiple satellites, your receiver can mathematically narrow down your exact location.

In the real 3D world, one satellite puts you on the surface of a sphere. Two satellites narrow it down to the circle where two spheres intersect. Three satellites narrow it down to two points. A fourth satellite (or the Earth's surface) identifies the correct point.

3. Time is Everything

How does the receiver know the distance? It calculates the time it took for a radio signal to travel from the satellite at the speed of light.

Distance = Velocity (Speed of Light) × Time Delay
c ≈ 300,000 km/s

Because the speed of light is so high, an error of just one microsecond (0.000001s) results in a position error of 300 meters! This is why satellites carry Atomic Clocks.

4. Relativistic Corrections

Without Albert Einstein's theories of relativity, GPS would fail within minutes:

  1. Special Relativity: Because satellites move fast (14,000 km/h), their clocks tick slower than clocks on Earth by about 7 microseconds/day.
  2. General Relativity: Because satellites are further from Earth's gravity, their clocks tick faster by about 45 microseconds/day.

The net result is that satellite clocks tick 38 microseconds faster per day. Engineers must offset the clock frequencies before launch to compensate!
