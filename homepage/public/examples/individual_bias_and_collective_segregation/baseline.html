The Segregation Paradox

Exploring how small individual preferences lead to large-scale social divides.

1. The Core Concept

In 1971, economist Thomas Schelling developed a simple model to explain a disturbing social phenomenon: why neighborhoods remain highly segregated even when individuals express a desire for diversity.

The "Schelling Model" demonstrates that collective segregation does not necessarily require extreme individual prejudice. It shows that even if every person is comfortable being in a minority (e.g., they only want 30% or 40% of their neighbors to be like them), the aggregation of these mild preferences leads to almost total residential segregation.

3. Observations & Implications

  • The "Tipping Point": When the preference threshold is set at 30%, you might expect a diverse mix. However, the system usually settles into blocks of color where the average similarity is over 70%.
  • Micro-motives vs. Macro-behavior: The final state of the system is often far more extreme than any single individual intended. This is known as an emergent property.
  • The Difficulty of Reversing Segregation: Once these clusters form, they are remarkably stable. Even if individuals become more tolerant, the spatial patterns persist until a major disruption occurs.

4. Educational Summary

The lesson of individual bias and collective segregation is one of complexity. It teaches us that to solve systemic social issues like segregation, it is often not enough to change individual hearts and minds alone; we must also look at the structural dynamics and the "rules of the game" that drive self-reinforcing patterns.
