The Art of Compass & Straightedge

Exploring the Foundations of Euclidean Geometry

1. The Rules of the Game

In Classical Geometry, we limit ourselves to just two tools. These restrictions were established by ancient Greek mathematicians like Euclid around 300 BCE. The challenge lies in creating complex shapes using only these simple devices:

  • The Straightedge: Used only to draw a straight line through two points. It has no markings (no ruler).
  • The Compass: Used to draw a circle with a given center passing through a given point. Once lifted, "collapsible" compasses (in the strict Euclidean sense) cannot keep their radius, though Euclid proved we can transfer distances effectively anyway.

3. Essential Constructions

Mastering geometry requires learning "recipes." Here are the most fundamental ones:

Perpendicular Bisector

  1. Draw a line segment between points A and B.
  2. Set the compass to a width greater than half the segment.
  3. Draw two circles of equal radius, one centered at A and one at B.
  4. The two points where the circles intersect define a new line.
  5. This line is perpendicular to the original and cuts it exactly in half.

Angle Bisector

  1. With the vertex as the center, draw an arc that crosses both rays of the angle.
  2. From the two new intersection points, draw two circles of equal radius.
  3. Connect the vertex to the point where these two circles intersect.

4. The Impossible Challenges

For over 2,000 years, mathematicians tried to solve three specific puzzles using only these tools. In the 19th century, it was finally proven that they are mathematically impossible:

  • Squaring the Circle: Constructing a square with the exact same area as a given circle. (Requires π, which is transcendental).
  • Doubling the Cube: Constructing a cube with twice the volume of a given cube. (Requires the cube root of 2).
  • Trisecting the Angle: Dividing an arbitrary angle into three equal parts.
