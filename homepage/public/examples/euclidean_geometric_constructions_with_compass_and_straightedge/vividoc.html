Euclidean geometric constructions with compass and straightedge

At the heart of Euclidean geometry lies a minimalist philosophy: complex figures are not "drawn" in the modern sense, but are instead constructed using a strictly limited set of moves. These moves are governed by the first three postulates of Euclid’s Elements, which define what is possible using only a straightedge (an unmarked ruler) and a collapsing compass.

The first operation is the ability to draw a straight line segment between any two distinct points $A$ and $B$. In this system, the straightedge serves no purpose for measurement; it exists solely as a physical guide to connect two spatial coordinates. Once these points are established, the segment $\overline{AB}$ becomes a rigid foundation for further geometric exploration.

The second operation involves extension. According to Euclidean principles, any finite line segment can be produced, or extended, continuously in a straight line. This allows us to transform a segment $\overline{AB}$ into a ray or an infinite line $\overleftrightarrow{AB}$. This is crucial when we need to find intersections between lines that originate far from one another.

The third and perhaps most versatile operation is the construction of a circle. Given any point $P$ as a center and any point $Q$ to define the radius, a circle can be drawn such that every point on the circumference is at an equal distance $r$ from the center $P$. Mathematically, this operation allows us to "transfer" distances and find the locus of all points satisfying the equation:

$(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2$

By mastering these three primitive actions—connecting points, extending lines, and swinging arcs—we gain the ability to locate new points through the intersection of these paths. These intersections are the only "legal" way to create new data in a construction, serving as the building blocks for every theorem and shape in the Greek tradition.

With the fundamental operations established, we can begin to synthesize them into more complex structures. The perpendicular bisector is arguably the most essential compound construction in Euclidean geometry; it allows us to find the exact midpoint of a segment $\overline{AB}$ and generate a line that meets it at a perfect $90^\circ$ angle without the use of a protractor or marked ruler.

The process relies entirely on the principle of equidistance. By using the length of the segment $AB$ as a radius, we construct two congruent circles—one centered at $P_1 = A$ and the other at $P_2 = B$. Because these circles share the same radius $r = AB$, they are symmetric reflections of one another. The two points where these circles intersect, let us call them $C$ and $D$, are mathematically significant: they are the only points on the plane that are simultaneously at distance $r$ from both $A$ and $B$.

By invoking Postulate 1 to draw a line through these new intersections $\overleftrightarrow{CD}$, we create the perpendicular bisector. This line functions as the axis of symmetry for the segment. Every point on $\overleftrightarrow{CD}$ satisfies the condition that its distance to point $A$ is equal to its distance to point $B$. Where this line intersects the original segment, it identifies the midpoint $M$, effectively partitioning $\overline{AB}$ into two equal segments such that $AM = MB$.

This construction elegantly demonstrates how the "collapsing" nature of the Euclidean compass is bypassed. Even though the tool itself cannot "carry" a measurement across the page, the intersections of these rigid arcs lock the geometry into a configuration that is logically and spatially undeniable. In this way, the perpendicular bisector serves as the gateway to constructing squares, circumcircles, and deep symmetry.

If the perpendicular bisector is the tool for partitioning linear space, the angle bisector is its rotational counterpart. To bisect an angle means to construct a ray that divides the original angle $\angle AOB$ into two congruent angles, such that $\angle AOC = \angle COB$. This technique rests on the principle of creating a rhombus or an equilateral kite, whose diagonal naturally serves as the axis of angular symmetry.

The construction begins by placing the compass at the vertex $O$ and swinging an arc of any radius $r$. This arc intersects the two rays of the angle at points $P_1$ and $P_2$. Because both points lie on the same arc centered at $O$, we have established that $OP_1 = OP_2$. These points serve as new, equidistant anchors for the final stage of the construction. In the Euclidean mindset, we have effectively "mapped" the divergence of the two lines onto two points at an identical distance from the origin.

From these two new anchors, $P_1$ and $P_2$, we draw two more arcs of equal radius. These arcs must have a radius large enough to intersect; typically, using the distance $P_1P_2$ is a safe choice. The point where these two arcs meet, point $C$, is technically the fourth vertex of a geometric figure where all sides (or at least adjacent pairs) are equal. Since point $C$ is equidistant from both $P_1$ and $P_2$, it must lie on the line of symmetry that passes through the vertex $O$.

By connecting the vertex $O$ to this intersection $C$ using a straightedge, we produce the bisecting ray. Mathematically, any point $(x, y)$ on this ray $\overrightarrow{OC}$ satisfies the property that its perpendicular distance to ray $\overrightarrow{OA}$ is equal to its perpendicular distance to ray $\overrightarrow{OB}$. This construction is vital for finding the incenter of a triangle—the unique point where the three internal angle bisectors meet—proving that even the most complex radial balances can be struck using only the intersection of simple circles.
