An algorithm for packing circles into containers

The two-dimensional circle packing problem is a classic puzzle in computational geometry and optimization. At its simplest, the goal is to arrange a set of $n$ circles of radii $r_i$ into a target container—such as a square, rectangle, or a larger circle—without any two circles crossing paths. This task is governed by two fundamental constraints that any algorithm must satisfy:

1. Non-overlap Constraint: For any two circles $i$ and $j$ with centers $(x_i, y_i)$ and $(x_j, y_j)$, the Euclidean distance between their centers must be at least the sum of their radii: $\sqrt{(x_i - x_j)^2 + (y_i - y_j)^2} \ge r_i + r_j$.

2. Boundary Constraint: Every point $(x, y)$ within a circle must lie within the geometric bounds of the container. For a circular container of radius $R$ centered at the origin, this is expressed as: $\sqrt{x_i^2 + y_i^2} \le R - r_i$.

The primary objective in these problems is typically to maximize the packing fraction ($\eta$), which represents the density of the arrangement. This is defined as the ratio of the total area of the packed circles to the total area of the container:

$$\eta = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \pi r_i^2}{Area_{container}}$$

Unlike tiles or squares, which can "tessellate" or fit together perfectly with zero wasted space, the geometry of circles introduces a persistent challenge: interstitial gaps. Because of their curved edges, even the most efficient arrangements—such as the hexagonal lattice—will always leave empty pockets. In an infinite plane, the maximum possible packing density for identical circles is $\eta \approx 0.9069$, meaning at least $9\%$ of the space will always remain empty. In constrained containers, this efficiency is often much lower, as the "hard" edges of the container force further compromises in the arrangement.

When the number of circles increases, finding a globally optimal arrangement becomes computationally expensive. To manage this complexity, we often employ a Greedy Iterative Placement heuristic. This algorithm simplifies the problem by making a series of locally optimal choices: it places one circle at a time into the container, fixing its position before moving on to the next. The "greedy" nature of the approach stems from the fact that it never reconsider decisions once a circle is placed, even if moving a previously placed circle would allow for a better overall fit later.

The efficiency of this heuristic is highly sensitive to the sorting order of the input set. If circles are of varying sizes, the most effective strategy is typically the Largest-First (LF) rule. By placing the largest circles first, the algorithm establishes the "anchor" points of the packing. The subsequent smaller circles then act as fillers, migrating into the irregular interstitial gaps left behind. If one were to reverse this—placing small circles first—the resulting "clutter" often prevents larger circles from finding a valid coordinate within the container later in the process.

To determine the specific coordinates $(x_i, y_i)$ for each new circle, the algorithm typically performs a search to find a position that minimizes a specific cost function. Common strategies include:

  • Distance Minimization: Placing the circle as close as possible to the container's center or a specific corner.
  • Touching Constraints: Identifying a position where the new circle $i$ is tangent to at least two existing boundaries (either two already-placed circles, or one circle and the container wall). This minimizes the local wasted area.

Mathematically, for each new circle $i$, we seek to minimize the distance to the origin $d_i = \sqrt{x_i^2 + y_i^2}$ subject to the non-overlap constraint $\sqrt{(x_i - x_j)^2 + (y_i - y_j)^2} \ge r_i + r_j$ for all $j < i$. While this greedy approach does not guarantee the maximum possible density $\eta$, it provides a remarkably fast and intuitive approximation that serves as the foundation for more advanced iterative refinement techniques.

While greedy placement provides a functional baseline, maximizing density requires a more structured geometric approach known as the Front-Chain or Advancing Front algorithm. Instead of searching the entire container for a valid coordinate, this method treats the existing packing as a growing "cluster." Each new circle is placed in a tight-coupling position—specifically, at a coordinate where it is simultaneously tangent to two already-placed circles. This ensures that the new circle occupies the deepest possible "pocket" in the current arrangement, effectively minimizing the area of the interstitial gaps.

The algorithm maintains a dynamic data structure called the Front: a chain of externally accessible circles that form the outer boundary of the cluster. When a new circle $C_n$ is added, the algorithm identifies an edge $(C_i, C_j)$ on the front and calculates a potential position $(x_n, y_n)$ representing the vertex of an apollonian gasket. Mathematically, the center of the new circle must satisfy a system of two quadratic equations based on the distance to the centers of its neighbors:

$$(x_n - x_i)^2 + (y_n - y_i)^2 = (r_n + r_i)^2$$ $$(x_n - x_j)^2 + (y_n - y_j)^2 = (r_n + r_j)^2$$

By solving this system, the algorithm finds the precise point where the three circles meet in mutual tangency. Once a circle is placed, the "front" is updated: the edge between $C_i$ and $C_j$ is removed, and two new edges, $(C_i, C_n)$ and $(C_n, C_j)$, are added to the chain. This creates a continuous, expanding frontier of potential placement slots.

This local optimization strategy significantly improves the packing fraction $\eta$ because it enforces the "highest-density" local configuration—the triangle—as the building block of the entire structure. However, the order of placement remains critical. If the front becomes too jagged or "pinched," it can create narrow valleys that larger circles cannot enter. Therefore, the Front-Chain algorithm is often paired with Areal Relaxation, where circles are allowed to slightly shift or "settle" into place, ensuring the chain remains as compact and concave as possible throughout the growth process.

While geometric heuristics like the Front-Chain algorithm are excellent for building dense clusters from the ground up, they often struggle with fixed boundaries or predefined sets of circles that do not fit perfectly into a growing chain. To overcome these rigidities, we can transition from static geometry to Force-Directed Physics. In this paradigm, we treat the circles as soft-body particles in a dynamic system governed by simulated physical laws. By assigning "forces" to the circles, we allow the entire arrangement to fluidly relax into a lower-energy state that naturally corresponds to a high-density packing.

The simulation usually relies on three primary forces operating simultaneously on each circle $i$:

  • Repulsive Overlap Force: When two circles $i$ and $j$ overlap, a repulsive force $\vec{F}_{rep}$ is applied along the vector connecting their centers. The magnitude is often proportional to the depth of the intrusion: $F_{rep} \propto \max(0, (r_i + r_j) - d_{ij})$. This pushes circles apart until the non-overlap constraint is satisfied.
  • Centripetal Attraction: To ensure the circles don't simply fly off into infinity, a global attractive force $\vec{F}_{attr}$ pulls every circle toward the center of the container or the origin: $\vec{F}_{attr} = -k \cdot \vec{p}_i$, where $\vec{p}_i$ is the position vector and $k$ is a stiffness constant.
  • Boundary Constraint Force: If a circle crosses the container wall, a restorative force $\vec{F}_{wall}$ pushes it back into the valid play area.

Mathematically, the algorithm updates the velocity $\vec{v}_i$ and position $\vec{x}_i$ of each circle over small time steps $\Delta t$ using a simplified version of Newton's Second Law ($\vec{F} = m\vec{a}$). The total force $\vec{F}_{total} = \sum \vec{F}_{rep} + \vec{F}_{attr} + \vec{F}_{wall}$ is used to calculate the shift in position:

$$\vec{x}_i(t + \Delta t) = \vec{x}_i(t) + \vec{v}_i(t)\Delta t + \frac{1}{2}\vec{a}_i(t)\Delta t^2$$

Through hundreds of iterations, the system undergoes stochastic cooling or damping, where the kinetic energy is gradually removed. As the velocities drop to zero, the circles settle into the "cracks" of the arrangement, often discovering unique configurations—such as the five-fold symmetry of quasicrystals or hexagonal lattices—that purely greedy algorithms might miss. This global optimization effectively "shakes" the container, allowing the circles to explore the phase space until they find the most compact collective equilibrium.
