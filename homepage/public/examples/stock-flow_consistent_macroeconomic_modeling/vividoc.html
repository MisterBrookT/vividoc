Stock-flow consistent macroeconomic modeling

At the heart of Stock-Flow Consistent (SFC) modeling lies a rigorous commitment to corporate and national accounting: the principle that every monetary flow must have an origin and a destination. In traditional macroeconomics, models often "leak" or have "black holes" where money disappears or appears from nowhere. SFC modeling eliminates these inconsistencies by ensuring that the financial world is treated as a closed system where everything must be accounted for.

To understand this framework, we must first distinguish between Stocks and Flows. A flow is a magnitude measured over a specific interval of time—think of your monthly salary or your annual spending. In contrast, a stock is a quantity measured at a specific point in time, representing an accumulation of past flows. Common examples of stocks include your total bank balance, the outstanding national debt, or the physical capital in a factory.

The most intuitive way to visualize this relationship is through the Water Tank Analogy. Imagine a water tank representing a Stock (such as Wealth, $W$). The water pouring in from the faucet represents an Inflow (such as Income, $Y$), and the water exiting through the drain represents an Outflow (such as Consumption, $C$):

$$\frac{\Delta W}{\Delta t} = Y - C$$

This fundamental accounting identity dictates that the change in a stock over a period is exactly equal to the net flow during that period. If you earn $\$5,000$ this month and spend $\$4,000$, your stock of wealth must increase by exactly $\$1,000$. There is no "third option."

In an SFC model, this logic is extended to the entire economy. If one sector (e.g., Households) is accumulating a stock of assets, another sector (e.g., Government or Firms) must be accumulating a corresponding stock of liabilities. By tracking these relationships through interconnected balance sheets, SFC models ensure that the model behaves according to the laws of "double-entry bookkeeping," providing a much more realistic simulation of how money actually moves through a modern economy.

If the water tank analogy illustrates the relationship between stocks and flows for a single entity, the Transaction Flow Matrix (TFM) scales this logic to the entire macroeconomy. In an SFC framework, we organize the economy into distinct Sectors—typically Households, Firms, Government, and the Rest of the World. The TFM is a rigorous accounting grid where every row represents a type of transaction (like consumption, taxes, or bond purchases) and every column represents a sector. Because every payment made by one sector is simultaneously a receipt for another, the sum of every row must be zero.

This "zero-sum" constraint is the mathematical expression of the law that money cannot be created or destroyed within the payment system—it can only be transferred. For instance, if the Government sector makes a payment for social services (an outflow, $-G$), that exact amount must appear as an inflow ($+G$) in the Household or Firm columns. When we sum the net flows for any single sector, we arrive at that sector's Net Lending or Financial Balance. If a sector’s total inflows exceed its total outflows, it is running a surplus and accumulating financial assets.

The most profound implication of this matrix is the Sectoral Balance Identity. By summing the balances of all sectors, we find that they must aggregate to zero. In a simplified closed economy (ignoring the foreign sector for a moment), the relationship between the Private Sector Balance ($S - I$) and the Public Sector Balance ($G - T$) is defined as:

$$(S - I) + (G - T) = 0$$

Rearranging this identity reveals a fundamental truth often overlooked in political discourse: $(S - I) = (T - G)$. This means that for the private sector to net save (run a surplus), the government must run an equivalent deficit. To use the water tank imagery, if the government "tank" is draining water out into the system, that water must necessarily be filling up the tanks of the private households and firms. One sector's red ink is, by accounting necessity, another sector's black ink.

In an open economy, we simply add the Foreign Sector (the Current Account). If a nation runs a trade deficit, it is essentially "importing" demand while "exporting" financial claims. An SFC model tracks these movements across the matrix, ensuring that no sector's financial position is analyzed in isolation. You cannot change the trajectory of the government's debt without simultaneously causing a mathematical shift in the wealth of the private or foreign sectors.

While the previous sections established the accounting "skeleton" of an SFC model, we must now add the "muscles"—the behavioral equations that determine how sectors react to changes in their financial environment. In a standard SFC model, the most critical behavioral link is the Consumption Function. Unlike simpler models where spending depends solely on current income, an SFC household looks at both its current flow of after-tax income ($YD$) and its accumulated stock of net wealth ($W$):

$$C = \alpha_1 \cdot YD + \alpha_2 \cdot W_{-1}$$

In this equation, $\alpha_1$ represents the marginal propensity to consume out of income, while $\alpha_2$ represents the marginal propensity to consume out of wealth. The term $W_{-1}$ denotes the stock of wealth at the end of the previous period. This creates a Dynamic Feedback Loop: as households save a portion of their income, their wealth grows; this higher wealth then stimulates more consumption in the next period, which in turn increases the income of firms and other households.

This relationship is what allows SFC models to reach a Steady State. If a shock occurs—such as a permanent increase in government spending—the economy does not simply jump to a new equilibrium. Instead, it undergoes a series of "stock-flow adjustments." Initially, the extra income leads to higher savings, causing private wealth to rise. As wealth accumulates, the "wealth effect" ($\alpha_2 \cdot W_{-1}$) pushes consumption higher and higher until the stock of wealth reaches a level where the outflows (spending) exactly offset the inflows (income). Only when the change in the stock ($\Delta W$) returns to zero has the system stabilized.

Tracking these dynamics is essential for understanding financial shocks. Consider a sudden change in interest rates. An increase in rates provides more interest income to bond-holding households (increasing $YD$), but it may simultaneously decrease the market value of existing assets (decreasing $W$). An SFC model captures both sides of this coin: the positive flow effect and the negative stock effect. By simulating these transitions over multiple periods, we can visualize the "path" the economy takes, ensuring that we never lose track of the debt or assets being created during the journey toward a new equilibrium.
