Spring–Mass Oscillator

From Hooke's restoring force to resonance catastrophe — the equations of oscillatory motion govern everything from atomic bonds to suspension bridges.

Section 01

Simple Harmonic Motion

Attach a mass \(m\) to a spring of stiffness \(k\), pull the mass aside by amplitude \(A\), and release. The spring pulls back proportionally to displacement: \(F = -kx\). Newton's second law then gives \(m\ddot{x} + kx = 0\), a second-order linear ODE whose general solution is \(x(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t + \varphi)\), with \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}\) rad/s. The angular frequency \(\omega_0\) — and hence the period \(T = 2\pi/\omega_0\) — depends only on the spring-to-mass ratio. A stiffer spring or lighter mass oscillates faster; a softer spring or heavier mass oscillates slower. Crucially, neither the period nor the frequency depends on amplitude. This isochronism is what makes the oscillator useful as a clock.

Energy is the other lens. The total mechanical energy \(E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2\) is fixed throughout the motion (no friction). At maximum displacement \(x = A\), all energy is elastic potential; the mass is instantaneously at rest. At the equilibrium position \(x = 0\), all energy is kinetic; the mass moves at its maximum speed \(v_{\max} = A\omega_0\). Between these extremes, energy sloshes back and forth between \(\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2\) and \(\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\). The position and velocity are \(90°\) out of phase — when \(x = A\cos(\omega_0 t)\), the velocity is \(v = -A\omega_0\sin(\omega_0 t)\).

Plotting \(x\) against \(v\) traces out an ellipse in phase space — a closed curve whose size encodes the total energy. A larger amplitude means a larger ellipse. Friction shrinks the ellipse inward spirally. An undamped oscillator traverses its ellipse forever, making exactly \(f_0 = \omega_0/(2\pi)\) full circuits per second. Section 02

Damped Oscillations

Real springs and masses lose energy to friction, air resistance, and internal dissipation. We model all of these as a velocity-proportional drag force \(-b\dot{x}\), giving the damped oscillator equation \(m\ddot{x} + b\dot{x} + kx = 0\). The character of the solution changes radically depending on how large the damping coefficient \(b\) is relative to the critical value \(b_c = 2\sqrt{km}\). We define the dimensionless damping ratio \(\zeta = b / b_c = b/(2\sqrt{km})\), and the natural frequency \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}\).

When \(\zeta < 1\) — the underdamped regime — the system oscillates but with exponentially shrinking amplitude: \(x(t) = Ae^{-\zeta\omega_0 t}\cos(\omega_d t + \varphi)\), where the damped frequency \(\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\) is slightly lower than \(\omega_0\). The exponential factor \(e^{-\zeta\omega_0 t}\) forms a decaying envelope that hugs the oscillation from above and below. When \(\zeta = 1\) — critically damped — the system returns to equilibrium in the shortest possible time without oscillating, following \(x(t) = (C_1 + C_2 t)e^{-\omega_0 t}\). When \(\zeta > 1\) — overdamped — the system returns even more sluggishly, and critically, never overshoots. Car suspension systems are typically designed close to \(\zeta \approx 0.7\), a balance between rapid settling and minimal oscillation.

The three regimes aren't just mathematically distinct — they're physically observable. Tap an underdamped wine glass and it rings for seconds. A door closer that slams is underdamped; one that drifts shut frustratingly slowly is overdamped; a well-adjusted one that swings firmly closed without bouncing is critically damped. Section 03

Driven Oscillator and Resonance

Add a periodic driving force \(F(t) = F_0\cos(\Omega t)\) to a damped oscillator, and after the transient decays, the system settles into a steady-state oscillation at the driving frequency \(\Omega\). The steady-state amplitude is \(A(\Omega) = (F_0/m) / \sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2)^2 + (2\zeta\omega_0\Omega)^2}\). This is the frequency response function — arguably the most important equation in applied mechanics. It tells you how vigorously a system responds to excitation at each frequency. When \(\Omega = 0\) (static load), \(A = F_0/(m\omega_0^2) = F_0/k\), which is just Hooke's law. As \(\Omega\) increases toward \(\omega_0\), the denominator shrinks and \(A\) grows dramatically.

The resonance peak occurs at \(\Omega_r = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}\) (slightly below \(\omega_0\) for nonzero damping). For small \(\zeta\), the peak amplitude is approximately \(F_0/(2\zeta\, m\omega_0^2)\), and the quality factor \(Q = 1/(2\zeta)\) measures the sharpness of the resonance peak — both how large the peak amplitude is and how narrow the peak frequency band is. A tuning fork has \(Q \sim 1000\); a guitar string \(Q \sim 200\); a car suspension \(Q \sim 0.7\). The famous 1940 Tacoma Narrows bridge collapse involved aeroelastic forcing near a structural resonance at low effective damping.

Equally important is the phase lag \(\delta = \arctan(2\zeta\omega_0\Omega / (\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2))\). Below resonance, the mass follows the force nearly in phase. At resonance, the response lags the force by exactly \(90°\) — the mass moves through equilibrium just as the force is maximum, maximizing energy transfer. Above resonance, the phase lag approaches \(180°\) — the mass moves opposite to the driving force, and the response amplitude falls off as \(\Omega^{-2}\).
