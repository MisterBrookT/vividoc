Double Pendulum

Two linked pendulums. Fully deterministic — governed by Newton's laws. Yet completely unpredictable beyond a few seconds. The simplest system that is genuinely chaotic.

01 — Equations of Motion

The Lagrangian

The double pendulum has two degrees of freedom: angles θ₁ and θ₂. Unlike the single pendulum, it has no closed-form solution for arbitrary amplitudes. The Lagrangian formulation — L = T − V — gives equations of motion that couple the two angles nonlinearly through inertia, gravity, and the constraint forces between the rods. This coupling is what makes the system so rich: energy flows between the two bobs in irregular bursts, punctuated by sudden flips of the lower bob.
The pivot is fixed. Rod 1 has length l₁ and carries mass m₁; rod 2 has length l₂ and carries mass m₂. The kinetic energy of m₂ must account for the combined motion of both rods. The resulting equations of motion are a pair of coupled second-order ODEs with no analytic solution — we integrate them numerically using 4th-order Runge–Kutta (RK4).
\[T = \tfrac{1}{2}(m_1+m_2)l_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}m_2 l_2^2\dot\theta_2^2 + m_2 l_1 l_2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\] \[V = -(m_1+m_2)g l_1\cos\theta_1 - m_2 g l_2\cos\theta_2\] Equations of motion from \(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot\theta_i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_i} = 0\) — coupled, nonlinear, no closed form.

Why RK4? Euler integration accumulates energy errors that make the pendulum spin faster over time (non-physical). RK4 evaluates four slope estimates per step and achieves 4th-order accuracy — errors scale as dt⁴. With dt = 0.005 s the energy drift over 60 seconds is less than 0.01%.
02 — Chaos and Sensitivity

Exponential Divergence

Start two identical double pendulums with θ₁ differing by just ε = 0.001 radians — about 0.06°. At first they track each other almost perfectly. Then, after a few seconds, they abruptly diverge and become completely uncorrelated. This exponential separation of initially nearby trajectories is the hallmark of deterministic chaos. The system is fully predictable in principle (Newton's laws), but practically unpredictable because any measurement error — however tiny — grows without bound.
The rate of divergence is characterized by the Lyapunov exponent λ. If d(t) is the angular separation between the two trajectories at time t, then d(t) ≈ d₀·e^{λt} in the chaotic regime. On a log-scale plot, this appears as a straight line with slope λ. For the double pendulum at moderate energy, λ ≈ 1–7 per second depending on the energy level, meaning trajectories double their separation every 0.1–0.7 seconds.
Lyapunov exponent: \(\lambda = \lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\frac{d(t)}{d(0)}\)
Predictability horizon: \(t^* \approx \frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\frac{\Delta_{\max}}{\Delta_0}\)
For \(\Delta_0 = 10^{-3}\) rad and \(\Delta_{\max} = 1\) rad: \(t^* \approx \frac{6.9}{\lambda} \approx 2\text{–}7\text{ s}\)

03 — Phase Space

Poincaré Section

The full phase space of the double pendulum is 4-dimensional: (θ₁, θ̇₁, θ₂, θ̇₂). To visualize it, we take a Poincaré section: record the state (θ₂, θ̇₂) every time θ₁ = 0 with θ̇₁ > 0. This strobes the 4D flow onto a 2D plane, revealing the system's global structure.
At low energy, the system is near-integrable: the Poincaré section shows smooth closed curves — invariant tori. As energy increases, these tori break up. Chaotic trajectories fill 2D regions with scattered dots; regular trajectories trace closed curves. The mix of smooth curves and scattered regions is characteristic of KAM theory (Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser): a fraction of the tori survive perturbation, and the rest break into chaos.
Section condition: record \((\theta_2,\,\dot\theta_2)\) when \(\theta_1 = 0\) and \(\dot\theta_1 > 0\)

KAM Theory: Andrei Kolmogorov (1954) proved that for small perturbations of an integrable system, most invariant tori survive — they are deformed but unbroken. Vladimir Arnold and Jürgen Moser extended this to nearly-integrable systems and dissipative maps. The double pendulum at moderate energy is far from the small-perturbation regime, so most tori have broken, but pockets of regularity persist — visible as the smooth curves in the Poincaré section.
