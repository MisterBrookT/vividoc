Section 01

The Pricing Formula

A European call option gives its holder the right — but not the obligation — to purchase an underlying asset at a predetermined strike price \(K\) on a fixed expiry date \(T\). At expiration, the payoff is simply \(\max(S_T - K,\ 0)\): you exercise only if the asset's spot price \(S_T\) exceeds the strike, pocketing the difference. The profound question, and the one that earned Black, Scholes, and Merton the Nobel Prize, is: what is this contingent claim worth today, before we know \(S_T\)?

The Black–Scholes model answers this by assuming stock price dynamics follow geometric Brownian motion — equivalently, that continuously compounded log-returns are normally distributed with constant volatility \(\sigma\). Under the risk-neutral measure (achieved by continuously delta-hedging), the expected payoff is discounted at the risk-free rate \(r\). This yields the celebrated closed-form formula: \(C = S \cdot N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2)\), where \(N(\cdot)\) is the standard normal CDF. The term \(S \cdot N(d_1)\) can be read as the expected value of the asset conditional on exercise, and \(K e^{-rT} N(d_2)\) is the present value of the strike payment weighted by the risk-neutral probability of exercise.

The inputs driving this formula produce rich intuitions. Deep in-the-money options (\(S \gg K\)) behave almost like the stock itself — their value tracks the asset dollar-for-dollar. Deep out-of-the-money options approach zero. As time to expiry \(T\) or volatility \(\sigma\) increases, the option gains value because uncertainty creates upside potential while the liability is limited to zero. This asymmetric payoff structure — the core insight behind derivatives pricing — is captured entirely in the formula below. Use the sliders to explore how each parameter drives the call value \(C\).

Section 02

The Greeks

Options traders manage risk through the "Greeks" — partial derivatives of the option price with respect to its inputs. Delta, \(\Delta = N(d_1)\), measures the instantaneous sensitivity of the call price to the underlying: a delta of 0.6 means the option gains approximately $0.60 for each $1 rise in the stock. Delta serves as the theoretical hedge ratio: to create a delta-neutral portfolio, you short \(\Delta\) shares of stock for each long call. Crucially, delta is bounded in \([0, 1]\) for calls, approaching 1 deep in-the-money and 0 deep out-of-the-money — the S-shaped curve below makes this visually obvious.

Gamma, \(\Gamma = \tfrac{\phi(d_1)}{S\sigma\sqrt{T}}\), is the second derivative of the call price with respect to \(S\), or equivalently the rate of change of delta. It peaks sharply near the at-the-money point because that is where delta transitions most rapidly from 0 to 1. A high-gamma position means your hedge (delta) goes stale quickly as the stock moves — you must rebalance frequently. This "gamma scalping" is the essence of volatility trading: long gamma benefits from large moves regardless of direction.

Theta, \(\Theta\), captures time decay: as expiry approaches, the time value of an option erodes, all else equal — long options lose money from theta even when the stock is unchanged. Vega, \(\mathcal{V} = S\sqrt{T}\phi(d_1)\), measures sensitivity to volatility: an option with vega of 0.35 gains $0.35 in value for each 1-percentage-point increase in implied volatility. Together, the Greeks form a complete local-linear (and local-quadratic via gamma) description of how an option's value changes in any scenario.

Section 03

The Volatility Smile

One of the most important empirical violations of Black–Scholes is the volatility smile (or skew). If the model were perfectly correct, the implied volatility — obtained by inverting the BS formula for a market-observed option price — would be constant across all strikes. It is not. In equity markets, options with strikes far from the current spot price (both deep in-the-money and deep out-of-the-money) trade at implied volatilities significantly higher than at-the-money options, forming a characteristic U-shaped "smile" when plotted against moneyness \(K/S\).

The existence of the smile reveals that real-world log-returns have fatter tails than the normal distribution assumes. Extreme moves — stock market crashes, sudden earnings collapses — occur more frequently than Brownian motion predicts. Deep OTM puts serve as portfolio insurance against tail events; investors bid up their prices, inflating their implied volatility. The mathematical foundation is clear: inverting \(C_{\text{mkt}} = C_{\text{BS}}(\sigma)\) via Newton–Raphson, we get \(\sigma_{n+1} = \sigma_n - (C(\sigma_n) - C_{\text{mkt}}) / \mathcal{V}(\sigma_n)\), which converges rapidly because BS is smooth and monotone in \(\sigma\). The resulting implied volatility surface — which also varies with expiry (the "term structure") — is the practitioner's core tool for quoting options and hedging consistently.

A simple parametric smile model approximates the curve as \(\sigma(k) = \sigma_{\min} + \alpha(k - 1)^2\) where \(k = K/S\) is moneyness and \(\alpha\) controls the smile's curvature.
