A neuron either fires completely or not at all — there is no partial spike. Once the membrane crosses −55 mV, a self-amplifying Na⁺ flood drives voltage to +40 mV regardless of stimulus strength. This leads us to the ion channels responsible for that all-or-nothing behavior.

Na⁺ — Depolarization

Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open rapidly when threshold is crossed. Na⁺ floods in (E_Na ≈ +55 mV), pulling the membrane toward +40 mV. Inactivation gates close within 1 ms, stopping the influx.

K⁺ — Repolarization

Slower voltage-gated K⁺ channels open after Na⁺ inactivation. K⁺ flows out (E_K ≈ −77 mV), pulling voltage back below rest — a brief hyperpolarization (refractory period).

Na⁺ and K⁺ act as sequential relay runners: Na⁺ sprints first to depolarize, K⁺ follows to restore order. Their timing difference, not their magnitude, shapes the spike. This sequential gating is why threshold exists — and why crossing it is irreversible.

03 — Threshold Dynamics

Why All-or-Nothing?

Gating variable steady-states: \(m_\infty(V) = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-(V+40)/5}}\) , \(n_\infty(V) = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-(V+55)/14}}\)
Refractory period: After a spike, Na⁺ channels are inactivated and K⁺ channels are still open, making a second spike impossible (absolute refractory, ~1 ms) or requiring a larger stimulus (relative refractory, ~4 ms). This limits firing rates to ~500–1000 Hz and ensures one-way propagation along axons.

The sharp threshold is a race between positive and negative feedback: fast Na⁺ activation versus slow K⁺ and Na⁺ inactivation. Below threshold, the slower processes win and the perturbation decays; above it, Na⁺ wins explosively. This bistability is what makes neurons reliable digital switches inside analog tissue.
