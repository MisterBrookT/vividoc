DNA Replication

How a cell copies 3 billion base pairs with error rates below one in a billion. The molecular machinery of life's most fundamental process.

01 — The Double Helix Unwinds

Base Pairing and the Replication Fork

The DNA double helix is held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases: adenine pairs with thymine (2 H-bonds), and guanine pairs with cytosine (3 H-bonds). This specificity is the molecular basis of heredity — each strand is a complete template for the other. Chargaff's rules state that in any double-stranded DNA, [A]=[T] and [G]=[C], a fact Chargaff observed empirically in 1950, three years before Watson and Crick's structure.
Replication begins at specific sequences called origins of replication. In E. coli, there is one origin (oriC); in human cells, there are ~30,000, allowing the entire 6-billion-base genome to be copied in ~8 hours. At each origin, the enzyme helicase uses ATP hydrolysis to unwind the helix at ~1000 bp/second, creating a Y-shaped replication fork. Single-strand binding proteins (SSBPs) stabilize the unwound strands, and topoisomerase relieves the torsional stress ahead of the fork.

02 — Leading and Lagging Strands

The Directionality Constraint

DNA polymerase III can only synthesize DNA in the 5'→3' direction — it adds nucleotides to the 3'-OH end of the growing strand. This creates an elegant asymmetry at the replication fork: one template strand (3'→5') allows continuous synthesis, producing the leading strand. The other template (5'→3') must be copied in short segments moving away from the fork — these are Okazaki fragments, each 100–200 nt in eukaryotes (1000–2000 in prokaryotes).
Each Okazaki fragment begins with a short RNA primer (~10 nt) synthesized by primase, which DNA Pol III then extends. After synthesis, RNase H removes the RNA primers, DNA Pol I fills the gaps, and DNA ligase seals the nicks with phosphodiester bonds, using NAD⁺ (bacteria) or ATP (eukaryotes) as energy source. The net result: both strands are copied, but the lagging strand synthesis requires much more coordination.
E. coli replication speed: \(\sim 1000\) bp/s per fork, \(\sim 2\) forks → full genome (\(4.6 \times 10^6\) bp) in \(\sim 40\) min
Human: \(\sim 50\) bp/s per fork, \(\sim 30{,}000\) origins → \(6\times10^9\) bp in \(\sim 8\) h
Processivity: Pol III stays on template for \(\sim 5 \times 10^5\) bp per binding event (β-clamp)

Lagging Strand

Template: 5'→3'. Pol III synthesizes each Okazaki fragment away from the fork. Each fragment requires its own primer. Pol I removes primers; ligase seals nicks. ~10× more coordination.

03 — Fidelity and Error Correction

One Error per Billion Bases

Raw base-selection accuracy of DNA Pol III is approximately 1 error per 10⁵ bases — already remarkable, but insufficient for a 6-billion-base genome (60,000 errors per division). Three successive error-correction mechanisms reduce this by nine orders of magnitude. First, the polymerase's own proofreading activity (3'→5' exonuclease) catches misincorporated bases immediately, reducing errors ~100-fold. Then mismatch repair (MMR) scans the newly synthesized strand afterward, reducing errors another ~1000-fold.
The net result: approximately 1 mutation per 10⁹–10¹⁰ base pairs per replication. Over a human lifetime, each cell division in skin generates ~1–2 point mutations; stem cells in the colon divide ~800 times — roughly 1600 mutations by age 80, most in non-coding regions. Cancer arises when mutations hit driver genes. This is why error correction fidelity is directly tied to cancer risk, and why MMR-deficient tumors have dramatically elevated mutation loads (Lynch syndrome).

Telomere problem: DNA Pol III cannot replicate the very end of linear chromosomes (no room for a new primer). Telomerase extends the 3' overhang using its own RNA template, solving the end-replication problem in germ cells and stem cells. In somatic cells, telomeres shorten with each division — a molecular clock for cellular aging — and critically short telomeres trigger senescence or apoptosis.
