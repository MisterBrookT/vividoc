Information Theory · Shannon 1948

Shannon Entropy

A mathematical measure of uncertainty, surprise, and the fundamental limits of information compression. Every bit you transmit or store is governed by this single elegant quantity.

01 — Surprise and Information Content

What Does It Mean to Learn Something?

In 1948, Claude Shannon asked a deceptively simple question: how do you measure information? His answer reshaped communication, cryptography, and our understanding of computation. The key insight was that information is the same thing as surprise. If you already know something will happen, learning it happens tells you nothing. If an unlikely event occurs, you've learned a great deal.
Formally, if an event has probability \(p\), the information content (or self-information) of that event is \(I = -\log_2 p\) bits. This formula is not arbitrary — it's the unique function satisfying three axioms: information should be non-negative, a certain event (\(p=1\)) carries zero information, and information from independent events should add. Logarithms are the only functions satisfying all three.
Consider a fair coin: each outcome has \(p = 0.5\), so \(I = -\log_2(0.5) = 1\) bit. A loaded coin with \(p = 0.99\) for heads carries only \(I = -\log_2(0.99) \approx 0.014\) bits when heads occurs — you expected it! But tails, at \(p = 0.01\), carries \(I = -\log_2(0.01) \approx 6.6\) bits. Rare events are highly informative.
Entropy \(H\) is the expected information content — the average surprise across all possible outcomes. For two outcomes with probabilities \(p\) and \(1-p\): \(H = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p)\). This is maximized when \(p = 0.5\) (maximum uncertainty) and collapses to zero when \(p=0\) or \(p=1\) (complete certainty, no surprise).
\[I(x) = -\log_2 p(x) \text{ bits} \qquad H(X) = -\sum_x p(x)\log_2 p(x) = \mathbb{E}[-\log_2 p(X)]\]

Why bits? The base-2 logarithm is conventional because the natural unit of communication is a binary choice: yes/no, 0/1, on/off. In natural units (nats, base \(e\)) or trits (base 3) the formula is identical — only the scale changes. Shannon's genius was realizing that entropy doesn't depend on the physical substrate, only on the probability distribution.
02 — Entropy of a Discrete Distribution

Six Symbols, Infinite Possibilities

Real communication alphabets have more than two symbols. Consider a source that emits one of six symbols \(\{s_1, s_2, s_3, s_4, s_5, s_6\}\) with probabilities \(p_1, \ldots, p_6\). The entropy generalizes naturally: \(H = -\sum_{i=1}^6 p_i \log_2 p_i\). This measures the average information per symbol emitted — and crucially, it sets a hard lower bound on how compactly the source can be encoded.
Shannon's source coding theorem (1948) states that no lossless encoding scheme can compress a source below its entropy rate, but entropy is always achievable in the limit. If a source emits symbols at rate \(H\) bits/symbol, you need at least \(H\) bits per symbol to represent it faithfully — and you can get arbitrarily close to this bound with sufficiently long block codes.
The maximum entropy for \(n\) symbols is \(\log_2 n\), achieved when all symbols are equally probable. This makes intuitive sense: if every symbol is equally likely, each one is maximally surprising. For 6 symbols, \(\log_2 6 \approx 2.585\) bits. The efficiency \(\eta = H / \log_2 n\) measures how close the distribution is to this maximum. English text has roughly 4.1 bits/letter (out of \(\log_2 26 \approx 4.7\) bits max), giving \(\eta \approx 87\%\).
There is a beautiful connection to Boltzmann's statistical mechanics entropy \(S = k_B \ln W\). Both measure the logarithm of the number of equally-likely microstates. Shannon entropy is Boltzmann entropy in disguise, measured in bits rather than joules per kelvin. The Second Law of Thermodynamics and the impossibility of lossless super-compression are the same idea wearing different clothes.
\[H(X) = -\sum_{i=1}^n p_i \log_2 p_i \leq \log_2 n \quad \text{with equality iff } p_i = \tfrac{1}{n} \;\forall i\]

English letter entropy: Claude Shannon famously estimated the entropy of written English at roughly 1–1.5 bits per character (using human subjects to predict the next letter), while a first-order letter model gives about 4.1 bits. The gap reflects the enormous predictive power of linguistic context — knowing the previous few letters tells you a lot about the next one. This gap is precisely what LZ-based compressors exploit.
03 — Mutual Information and Channel Efficiency

What Can a Noisy Channel Carry?

So far, entropy has measured uncertainty about a single source. But communication is about two random variables: the input \(X\) and the output \(Y\). The fundamental question is: how much information about \(X\) can we recover from \(Y\) after the channel corrupts it? The answer is mutual information \(I(X;Y)\).
The simplest noisy channel is the Binary Symmetric Channel (BSC). Input is a single bit (\(X \in \{0,1\}\)). With probability \(\varepsilon\) the bit flips; with probability \(1-\varepsilon\) it passes through intact. If we input bits with equal probability (\(P(X=0)=0.5\)), then \(H(X) = H(Y) = 1\) bit. The noise introduces conditional entropy \(H(Y|X) = H_b(\varepsilon)\) — the remaining uncertainty in \(Y\) even after knowing \(X\).
Mutual information measures the reduction in uncertainty: \(I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y|X) = 1 - H_b(\varepsilon)\). When \(\varepsilon=0\) (perfect channel), \(I(X;Y) = 1\) bit — you learn \(X\) exactly. When \(\varepsilon=0.5\) (random flipping), \(H_b(0.5)=1\), so \(I(X;Y)=0\) — the channel is pure noise and transmits no information whatsoever.
The channel capacity \(C = \max_{p(X)} I(X;Y)\) is the maximum mutual information over all possible input distributions. For the BSC with parameter \(\varepsilon\), \(C = 1 - H_b(\varepsilon)\). Shannon's channel coding theorem (the most profound result in information theory) states that reliable communication at rate \(R < C\) is possible — there exist codes that drive the error probability to zero as block length grows. Above \(C\), error-free communication is impossible, regardless of how clever the encoding.
This theorem told engineers something startling: noise is not a fundamental obstacle to reliable communication, only a limit on rate. Error-correcting codes (Hamming codes, LDPC, Turbo codes) approach the Shannon limit. Modern 5G systems operate within fractions of a dB of capacity.

Why error-correcting codes work: Shannon's theorem guarantees existence but doesn't tell you how to build good codes. For 45 years after 1948, the best known codes left a large gap to capacity. Turbo codes (Berrou, 1993) and the LDPC revival (Richardson & Urbanke, 2001) finally bridged it. Today, LDPC codes are used in Wi-Fi (802.11n), DVB-S2 satellite TV, and 5G NR — all operating within 0.1–0.5 dB of Shannon capacity.
