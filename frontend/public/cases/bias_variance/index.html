Bias–Variance Tradeoff

Generalization · Overfitting · Regularization

Section 01

Polynomial Fitting and the Tradeoff

Imagine you are trying to learn a function from noisy data. With a very simple model — say, a straight line — you will systematically miss curves and bumps in the true function. This systematic error that persists no matter how much data you have is called bias. A line cannot fit a sine wave, regardless of the sample size.

With a very complex model — say, a degree-12 polynomial — you can fit any 15 data points exactly. But the model will memorize the noise, producing wild oscillations between data points. Change the random seed and you get a completely different polynomial. This sensitivity to the choice of training set is called variance. High-variance models are brittle: they generalize poorly to new data.

The fundamental decomposition of expected prediction error is: $\mathbb{E}[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2] = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \sigma^2$. The noise term $\sigma^2$ is irreducible — it reflects randomness in the data generating process. But bias and variance can be traded off against each other by choosing model complexity. As you increase degree $d$, bias falls (the model can represent more functions) while variance rises (it becomes more sensitive to the specific data). Total error is U-shaped with a sweet spot at the optimal complexity.

This is not just theory — it manifests concretely every time you fit a polynomial, train a decision tree, or choose the number of layers in a neural network. Increasing the degree from 1 to 4 dramatically reduces the systematic error on $\sin(2\pi x)$. But pushing to degree 10 or 12 causes Runge's phenomenon: extreme oscillations at the boundaries. Watch it happen below.

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2\right] = \underbrace{\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f(x)\right)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)])^2\right]}_{\text{Variance}} + \sigma^2$$

Section 02

The Bias–Variance Decomposition Plot

To truly understand the tradeoff, we need to look across all model complexities simultaneously. By fitting polynomials of degree 1 through 12 on many different random training sets, we can empirically estimate the bias and variance for each degree. Averaging predictions across datasets gives the expected prediction, from which we compute the squared deviation from the true function (bias²). The spread of predictions around their mean gives the variance.

The resulting plot reveals a striking structure. Training error decreases monotonically with degree — a more complex model always fits the training data better. But test error forms a U-shape: it decreases initially as the model learns the true signal, then increases as the model begins fitting noise. The gap between training and test error is the generalization gap, which grows with complexity.

Two parameters fundamentally shift the optimal complexity. With more training data ($N$ larger), the model has more information to distinguish signal from noise, so more complex models become viable — the optimal degree shifts right. With less noise ($\sigma$ smaller), the data is more informative per point, again allowing higher-complexity models to succeed. These intuitions drive practical deep learning decisions: larger models for larger datasets, and more regularization for noisier problems.

The double descent phenomenon — recently observed in neural networks — complicates this picture. At interpolation threshold (where the model exactly fits all training points), test error can spike dramatically, but then decrease again as the model becomes even more overparameterized. Modern large language models operate in this ultra-high-complexity regime, making the classical bias-variance story an approximation rather than the whole truth.

$$\text{MSE}_{\text{test}} \approx \text{Bias}^2(d) + \text{Var}(d) + \sigma^2 \qquad \text{(decomposed over degree } d \text{)}$$

Section 03

Regularization as Variance Reduction

When we cannot control model complexity directly — for instance, in a deep network where reducing depth would be too costly — we can penalize complexity implicitly through regularization. Ridge regression adds a term $\lambda \sum_j w_j^2$ to the loss function, discouraging large weights. This shrinks coefficients toward zero, effectively reducing the model's capacity to fit noise.

The Bayesian interpretation is elegant: regularization is equivalent to placing a Gaussian prior $p(\mathbf{w}) \propto \exp(-\lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|^2 / 2)$ on the weights. The L2 penalty encodes our prior belief that weights should be small. Large $\lambda$ encodes strong belief in small weights (high bias, low variance). Small $\lambda$ lets the data speak (low bias, high variance). The regularization strength $\lambda$ is thus a dial that directly controls the bias-variance tradeoff.

Lasso regression uses an L1 penalty $\lambda \sum_j |w_j|$ instead, which has a sparsifying effect: it drives many coefficients exactly to zero, effectively performing automatic feature selection. This corresponds to a Laplace prior on weights. In high-dimensional problems where only a few features are relevant, Lasso can dramatically outperform Ridge by eliminating irrelevant features entirely.

In neural networks, the equivalent of L2 regularization is called weight decay, appearing directly as a hyperparameter in optimizers like AdamW. The optimal $\lambda$ is found by cross-validation: fit the model on a training fold, evaluate on a validation fold, and choose the $\lambda$ that minimizes validation error. The resulting validation MSE curve has a characteristic valley — too little regularization overfits (right side), too much underfits (left side). The sweet spot is the bottom of the valley.

$$\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{ridge}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}} \sum_{i=1}^N \left(y_i - \mathbf{w}^\top \phi(x_i)\right)^2 + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|^2$$
