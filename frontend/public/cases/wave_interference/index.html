When two waves meet, they don't collide — they combine. Explore how amplitude, phase, and geometry sculpt the patterns that light and sound leave behind.

Section 01

The Principle of Superposition

Every undergraduate physics course introduces the superposition principle with something like: "the total displacement is the sum of the individual displacements." But the full depth of that statement only becomes apparent when you watch it happen. Two waves — each sinusoidal, each perfectly obedient to the wave equation — arrive at the same point, and the medium simply adds them. The medium has no memory of which wave contributed what; it simply responds to the net force.

Consider two traveling waves with possibly different amplitudes and phases: \(y_1 = A_1\sin(kx - \omega t + \varphi_1)\) and \(y_2 = A_2\sin(kx - \omega t + \varphi_2)\). Their sum is another sinusoid whose amplitude depends critically on the phase difference \(\Delta\varphi = \varphi_2 - \varphi_1\). When \(\Delta\varphi = 0\) (or any integer multiple of \(2\pi\)), the crests of one wave land exactly on the crests of the other — constructive interference — and the resultant amplitude is \(A_1 + A_2\). When \(\Delta\varphi = \pi\) (or any odd multiple), crests cancel troughs — destructive interference — leaving \(|A_1 - A_2|\). For equal amplitudes \(A_1 = A_2 = A\), destructive interference is complete annihilation.

The general resultant amplitude for equal amplitudes comes from the phasor addition rule. Adding two sinusoids of equal amplitude with phase difference \(\Delta\varphi\) yields a sinusoid with the amplitude below. This is not merely a computational formula — it encodes the transition from constructive (\(\Delta\varphi = 0\)) to destructive (\(\Delta\varphi = \pi\)) as a smooth cosine, which is why interference fringes in optics have a smooth intensity gradient rather than sharp on/off edges.

Phasor Resultant \[\text{For equal amplitudes: } y_1 + y_2 = 2A\cos\!\left(\frac{\Delta\varphi}{2}\right)\sin\!\!\left(kx - \omega t + \frac{\varphi_1+\varphi_2}{2}\right)\] \[\text{General case: } R = \sqrt{A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1 A_2\cos(\Delta\varphi)}\]

Section 02

Two-Source Interference in Two Dimensions

A single sinusoidal source sends out circular wavefronts in all directions — concentric rings expanding at the wave speed. Add a second identical source, separated by a distance \(d\), and the two families of concentric rings overlap. Where rings of the same phase meet, the medium heaves upward twice as high; where opposing phases meet, the medium sits still. The result is a stationary pattern of bright and dark fringes frozen in space, even as the waves themselves rush outward at speed \(v = \lambda f\).

The key variable governing which pattern forms at any given point P in space is the path difference \(\Delta r = r_2 - r_1\), the difference in distances from P to each source. Constructive interference occurs wherever \(\Delta r = n\lambda\) for integer \(n\); destructive wherever \(\Delta r = (n + \tfrac{1}{2})\lambda\). The locus of points with a fixed path difference forms a hyperbola, so the fringe pattern consists of a family of hyperbolic nodal lines radiating outward from the midpoint between the sources.

In the far field — when the observation screen is far from the sources, \(L \gg d\) — the hyperbolic fringes straighten into the familiar Young's double-slit pattern. The bright fringe positions on a screen at distance \(L\) are approximated by \(y_n \approx n\lambda L / d\), and the angular positions by \(\theta_n = \arcsin(n\lambda/d)\). The intensity in this approximation is \(I \propto \cos^2(\pi d \sin\theta / \lambda)\). Section 03

Beats and Standing Waves

When two waves of slightly different frequencies travel in the same direction, their superposition produces a phenomenon every musician knows: beats. The amplitude of the combined wave swells and fades at a rate equal to the difference in frequencies. A piano tuner exploits this directly — by listening for the beat frequency between a struck string and a reference tuning fork, she can reduce \(|f_1 - f_2|\) to zero, achieving perfect unison. The mathematics behind this is the same product-to-sum identity that gives us the carrier and envelope in amplitude modulation radio.

A standing wave, on the other hand, arises from two waves of the same frequency traveling in opposite directions. This happens naturally on a plucked guitar string: the traveling wave bounces off both fixed endpoints, and the two counter-propagating waves superpose into a pattern that appears frozen in space. The nodes — points of permanent zero displacement — are fixed at positions \(x = n\lambda/2\); the antinodes between them oscillate with doubled amplitude. What looks like a stationary pattern is actually a rapid oscillation that stroboscopic photography makes visible only at certain moments.

The allowed frequencies on a string of length \(L\) fixed at both ends form a harmonic series: \(f_n = nv/(2L)\), where \(v = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) is the wave speed set by the string tension \(T\) and linear mass density \(\mu\). The fundamental (\(n=1\)) has exactly one half-wavelength fitting between the fixed ends; the second harmonic (\(n=2\)) fits two; and so on. This integer sequence underlies the harmonic structure of virtually all musical instruments, from organ pipes to stretched membranes.

Beats and Standing Wave Formulas \[\text{Beats: } y_1+y_2 = 2A\cos\!\left(\pi\Delta f \cdot t\right)\cos\!\left(2\pi \bar{f}\, t\right), \quad f_{\rm beat} = |f_1 - f_2|\] \[\text{Standing wave: } y(x,t) = 2A\cos(kx)\sin(\omega t)\] \[\text{String harmonics: } f_n = \frac{n\,v}{2L}, \quad \lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}, \quad n = 1,2,3,\ldots\]
