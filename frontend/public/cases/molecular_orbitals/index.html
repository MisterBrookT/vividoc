Molecular Orbitals

When atomic orbitals combine, they form molecular orbitals that extend over the entire molecule. Explore bonding, antibonding states, MO diagrams for the second-row diatomics, and the frontier orbital theory that governs chemical reactivity.

01 — LCAO Theory

Bonding and Antibonding in H₂

The simplest quantum mechanical description of a covalent bond starts with Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO). When two hydrogen atoms approach each other, their 1s orbitals — described by wave functions ψ_A and ψ_B — begin to overlap. Since the Schrödinger equation is linear, we can form molecular orbitals by adding or subtracting these atomic orbitals. This is not merely a mathematical convenience: the resulting wave functions describe real, measurable electron distributions.
\[\psi_{\text{bond}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2+2S}}(\psi_A + \psi_B) \qquad \psi_{\text{anti}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2-2S}}(\psi_A - \psi_B)\]
In the bonding orbital (σ), the two wave functions add constructively between the nuclei. The electron probability density ψ² is enhanced in the internuclear region — electrons are shared and drawn toward both positive nuclei simultaneously, lowering the overall energy and binding the atoms together. This constructive interference is the quantum mechanical origin of the covalent bond. The bonding MO energy falls below that of either isolated atom.
In the antibonding orbital (σ*), the wave functions subtract, creating a nodal plane midway between the nuclei where ψ = 0 and therefore the electron probability is zero. Electron density is depleted from the internuclear region and concentrated outside the nuclei. This arrangement is electrostatically unfavorable: the nuclei repel each other with nothing to screen them. The antibonding MO energy rises above that of the isolated atoms. Placing electrons in σ* is destabilizing — a crucial fact that explains why He₂ does not form (2 bonding, 2 antibonding → bond order = 0) while H₂ does (2 bonding, 0 antibonding → bond order = 1).
The bond order — the effective number of bonds — is calculated as (bonding electrons − antibonding electrons) / 2. Bond order directly correlates with bond strength (dissociation energy) and inversely correlates with bond length. A bond order of zero means no stable bond exists; fractional bond orders (as in O₂) are physically meaningful and lead to remarkable magnetic properties.

Why does He₂ not exist? He has 2 electrons, so He₂ would have 4 electrons total. Filling in order: σ1s (2 bonding) then σ*1s (2 antibonding). Bond order = (2−2)/2 = 0. No net stabilisation — He₂ does not form. Meanwhile H₂ has bond order = 1, and its dissociation energy of 436 kJ/mol represents the energy gained by that single net bonding electron pair.
02 — MO Diagrams

Homonuclear Diatomics: Li₂ through F₂

For the second-row diatomic molecules, we combine the 2s and 2p atomic orbitals of both atoms. The 2s orbitals form σ₂s and σ*₂s molecular orbitals, while the 2p orbitals form three types: a σ₂p orbital along the bond axis, two degenerate π₂p orbitals perpendicular to it, and their antibonding counterparts σ*₂p, π*₂p×2, σ*₂p.
There is a subtle but important detail: for Li₂ through N₂, the σ₂p orbital is higher in energy than the π₂p orbitals — a counterintuitive ordering caused by mixing (hybridization) between the 2s and 2p orbitals of the same atom when they are energetically close. For O₂ and F₂, the s-p energy gap is large enough that this mixing is negligible, and the familiar ordering σ₂p < π₂p is restored. This orbital ordering switch is not just academic: it explains why O₂ is paramagnetic (two unpaired electrons) in a way that the incorrect ordering would not.
Electrons fill molecular orbitals following the same Aufbau principle as atomic orbitals: lowest energy first, two electrons per orbital (Pauli exclusion), and degenerate orbitals filled singly before pairing (Hund's rule). The resulting electron configurations directly determine bond order, bond length, bond strength, and magnetic properties. N₂ with bond order 3 has the strongest homonuclear diatomic bond (945 kJ/mol, 1.10 Å). O₂ with bond order 2 but two unpaired π* electrons is paramagnetic — attracted to a magnetic field. This was a famous failure of valence bond theory until MO theory correctly predicted it.

O₂ paramagnetism — MO theory's triumph: Valence bond theory draws O₂ with a double bond and all electrons paired. But liquid O₂ is attracted to a magnet — it is paramagnetic, meaning it has unpaired electrons. MO theory reveals why: the two π*₂p orbitals are degenerate, and Hund's rule places one electron in each, both with parallel spin. Bond order = (8−4)/2 = 2, consistent with the O=O double bond, but with two unpaired electrons that valence bond theory completely misses.
03 — Frontier Molecular Orbital Theory

HOMO–LUMO Gap and Reactivity

Among all molecular orbitals, two are uniquely important for chemical reactivity: the HOMO (Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital) and the LUMO (Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital). During a chemical reaction, electrons flow from the HOMO of one molecule to the LUMO of another. This is the central insight of Frontier Molecular Orbital (FMO) theory, developed by Kenichi Fukui (Nobel Prize 1981).
\[\Delta E \approx \frac{-(\text{HOMO}_{\text{nucleophile}} \to \text{LUMO}_{\text{electrophile}})^2}{E_{\text{HOMO}} - E_{\text{LUMO}}}\]
The HOMO-LUMO gap — the energy difference between HOMO and LUMO — determines how easily electrons can be promoted or transferred. A small gap means low-energy photons can excite the molecule (it absorbs visible light, making it colored), it is easily oxidised or reduced (electrochemically reactive), and it can participate in many chemical reactions. A large gap means the molecule is stable, transparent (absorbs only UV), and chemically inert under normal conditions. This is why N₂ is so unreactive despite its triple bond — the gap is enormous.
The HOMO-LUMO gap governs the color of organic dyes and pigments. Conjugated systems like beta-carotene (11 conjugated double bonds) have a small gap, absorbing blue light and appearing orange. Extending conjugation further pushes absorption into the red, then infrared. This principle guides the design of organic solar cells (OPV) and organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs), where tuning the gap controls the absorption spectrum and emission color. Polymer semiconductors like poly(3-hexylthiophene) with gaps around 1.9 eV absorb across much of the visible spectrum.
In solid-state physics, the HOMO-LUMO gap corresponds to the band gap between valence band and conduction band. Conductors have zero gap, semiconductors have a small gap (silicon: 1.1 eV, germanium: 0.67 eV), and insulators have a large gap (diamond: 5.5 eV). The same quantum mechanical principle — ease of electron promotion — unifies molecular reactivity, photochemistry, and solid-state electronics.

FMO in organic synthesis: The Diels-Alder reaction works because the HOMO of the diene and the LUMO of the dienophile have compatible symmetry and are close in energy. Electron-withdrawing groups on the dienophile lower its LUMO, narrowing the gap and accelerating the reaction. This is why maleic anhydride is a far better dienophile than ethylene — FMO theory quantitatively predicts relative reactivity in [4+2] cycloadditions without needing transition state calculations.
