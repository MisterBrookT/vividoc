FOURIER TRANSFORM

Decomposing signals into their harmonic components

Any periodic signal is built from sinusoidal harmonics at integer-multiple frequencies.

The Fourier Transform is one of the most powerful ideas in mathematics: every periodic waveform — no matter how sharp, jagged, or complex — is secretly a sum of pure sine waves. Each sine wave vibrates at a specific frequency, carries its own amplitude, and arrives at its own phase.

A square wave, a sawtooth, a triangle — each has a characteristic harmonic signature. The general formula for the Fourier series is:

$$ f(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left[ a_n \cos(2\pi n f_0 t) + b_n \sin(2\pi n f_0 t) \right] $$

Adding more harmonics approximates the target wave — but corners always cause a ~9% Gibbs overshoot.

Start with just the fundamental frequency and you have a sine wave. Add the 3rd harmonic (three times the frequency, a third of the amplitude) and the shape begins to square off. Keep adding odd harmonics — 5th, 7th, 9th — and the approximation sharpens dramatically.

But something strange happens at the sharp jumps. No matter how many terms you add, the approximation always overshoots by about 9%. This is the Gibbs phenomenon: a ringing artifact that never disappears, it only narrows. The partial sum is:

$$ S_N(t) = \frac{4}{\pi} \sum_{\substack{k=1 \\ k \text{ odd}}}^{N} \frac{1}{k} \sin(kt) $$

The overshoot converges to exactly $\frac{\text{Si}(\pi)}{\pi/2} - 1 \approx 8.9\%$, where $\text{Si}$ is the sine integral — a constant of nature, not a computational error. Drag the slider and watch the red Gibbs marker: it narrows but never vanishes.

The Fourier series is equivalent to a set of rotating circles — epicycles — whose tip traces the waveform.

There is a beautiful geometric interpretation hiding inside the Fourier series. Every harmonic can be visualized as a rotating circle (an epicycle): it spins at its harmonic frequency with a radius proportional to its amplitude. Mount the second circle on the tip of the first, the third on the tip of the second — and let them all rotate simultaneously.

The tip of the last circle traces the waveform. In complex notation:

$$ f(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n \, e^{i n \omega_0 t} $$

Each term $c_n e^{in\omega_0 t}$ is a phasor — a point rotating in the complex plane at frequency $n\omega_0$. This is exactly how Ptolemy modelled planetary orbits in 150 AD, and exactly how a Fourier synthesizer works. Add more epicycles and the traced curve converges to a square wave.
