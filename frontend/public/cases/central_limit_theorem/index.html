The Central Limit Theorem

No matter the shape of the original distribution, the sampling distribution of the mean converges to a normal as sample size grows. This is the most remarkable theorem in statistics.

01 — The Convergence

Sums Become Normal

Pick any distribution — even wildly skewed or discrete ones. Draw samples of size n, compute each sample's mean, and plot the results. As n grows, the histogram of those means converges to a perfect bell curve, regardless of the original shape.

02 — The Theorem

Formal Statement

Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\) be i.i.d. random variables with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\). The CLT says the standardized sample mean converges in distribution to the standard normal.
\[\sqrt{n}\;\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{\,d\,} \mathcal{N}(0,1) \quad \text{as } n\to\infty\] Equivalently: \(\bar{X}_n \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\; \dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\) approximately, for large \(n\).
The key insight: the variance of the sample mean is \(\sigma^2/n\), shrinking as \(1/\sqrt{n}\). Doubling the sample size only halves the standard error — to halve again you need four times the data.

03 — Why It Matters

The Foundation of Inference

The CLT is why most of classical statistics works even when we don't know the population distribution. It justifies t-tests, confidence intervals, and A/B testing at scale.

Confidence Intervals

A 95% CI for the population mean is \(\bar{x} \pm 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}\). This works because the CLT guarantees \(\bar{X}\) is approximately normal — regardless of the original distribution.

Hypothesis Testing

Z-tests and t-tests use the CLT to say: "if the null is true, how likely is an effect this large?" The normal approximation makes this tractable even for unknown population distributions.

The Square Root Law

Standard error ∝ 1/√n. To cut measurement uncertainty by 2×, you need 4× the data. Pollsters use this to calibrate sample sizes for ±3% margins.

Limits of the CLT

Requires finite variance. Heavy-tailed distributions (Cauchy, Pareto with α < 2) violate this — their means don't converge to normal. Financial returns near crashes live here.

Historical note: Pierre-Simon Laplace proved an early version of the CLT in 1810. The modern statement using "convergence in distribution" was formalized by Lyapunov (1901) and Lindeberg (1922). It underpins the frequentist interpretation of probability that dominates experimental science and clinical trials.
