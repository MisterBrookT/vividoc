01 — Integrated Rate Laws

Reaction Order and Concentration Decay

Every chemical reaction has a rate law: an equation relating the rate of consumption of reactants to their concentrations. For a simple reaction A → products, the rate law takes the form rate = k[A]ⁿ, where k is the rate constant and n is the reaction order — typically 0, 1, or 2. The order is an empirical quantity; it cannot generally be read off from the balanced equation, but must be determined by measuring concentration vs. time in the laboratory.
\[\frac{d[\text{A}]}{dt} = -k[\text{A}]^n\]
Integrating the rate law yields the integrated rate law — a direct expression for how concentration evolves with time. For a zeroth-order reaction (n = 0), the rate is constant and concentration declines linearly: [A](t) = [A]₀ − kt. The reaction proceeds at the same rate regardless of how much reactant remains; this occurs when the reaction is limited by a surface or enzyme that is always saturated. For a first-order reaction (n = 1), every molecule decomposes independently, giving exponential decay: [A](t) = [A]₀ · e^(−kt). Radioactive decay and many unimolecular reactions follow this law — each nucleus or molecule has a fixed probability per unit time of reacting. For a second-order reaction (n = 2), molecules must collide pairwise, so the rate slows dramatically as concentration drops: [A](t) = [A]₀/(1 + k[A]₀t).
One diagnostic tool is the half-life t₁/₂ — the time required for concentration to fall to half its initial value. For a zeroth-order reaction, t₁/₂ = [A]₀/(2k), which depends on initial concentration. For a first-order reaction, t₁/₂ = ln 2/k ≈ 0.693/k, which is gloriously independent of initial concentration — this is the familiar constant half-life of radioactive isotopes. For a second-order reaction, t₁/₂ = 1/(k[A]₀), inversely proportional to initial concentration. Experimenters routinely measure half-lives at several starting concentrations to determine reaction order.
To identify order experimentally, one plots [A] vs. t (linear for 0th order), ln[A] vs. t (linear for 1st order), or 1/[A] vs. t (linear for 2nd order) — whichever gives a straight line reveals the order. The slope of that line gives k. Use the controls below to explore how the shape of the decay curve changes with order, rate constant, and initial concentration.

02 — Arrhenius Equation

Temperature and the Energy Barrier

Rate constants are not truly constant — they depend exponentially on temperature. In 1889, Svante Arrhenius proposed an equation that has become one of the most important in all of physical chemistry. It explains why reactions speed up dramatically when heated, why food spoils faster in summer, and why enzymes evolved to work near 37 °C rather than 100 °C.
\[k = A\,e^{-E_a / RT}\]
The activation energy Eₐ is the minimum energy that colliding molecules must possess for a reaction to occur. Imagine a potential-energy surface: reactants sit in a valley, and the transition state is a mountain pass. Only molecules with enough kinetic energy to climb this barrier contribute to the reaction rate. The pre-exponential factor A (also called the frequency factor) captures both the collision frequency and the geometric requirement that molecules be correctly oriented when they collide. It has units of s⁻¹ for first-order reactions or M⁻¹s⁻¹ for second-order.
The exponential dependence on temperature is striking. At 310 K (near body temperature), a 10 K increase in temperature typically doubles the rate constant for reactions with typical Eₐ values of 50–70 kJ/mol. This "doubling rule" is a rough empirical observation — more precisely, the ratio k(T+10)/k(T) = exp(Eₐ × 10 / [R·T·(T+10)]). High-Eₐ reactions are more sensitive to temperature changes; low-Eₐ reactions (like enzyme-catalysed reactions, Eₐ ≈ 20–40 kJ/mol) are less sensitive. Catalysts work precisely by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy — the same Arrhenius exponential applies, but with a smaller Eₐ, giving an enormously larger k at the same temperature.
Taking the natural log of the Arrhenius equation gives ln k = ln A − Eₐ/(RT), so a plot of ln k vs 1/T (an "Arrhenius plot") is a straight line with slope −Eₐ/R. Chemists use Arrhenius plots to extract Eₐ experimentally: measure k at several temperatures, plot ln k vs 1/T, fit a line. The slope gives −Eₐ/R ≈ −Eₐ/8.314. This technique is used in drug stability testing, food science, and materials engineering.

Catalysis and Eₐ: A platinum catalyst reduces the Eₐ of H₂ + O₂ → H₂O from ~240 kJ/mol to ~50 kJ/mol, making the reaction proceed at room temperature instead of requiring an ignition source. Biological enzymes are even more dramatic — they reduce Eₐ by stabilising the transition state through precise geometric and electrostatic interactions in the active site. The Arrhenius factor improvement can be 10⁸ to 10²⁰ fold compared to uncatalysed reactions.
03 — Enzyme Kinetics

Michaelis–Menten Saturation

Unlike ordinary chemical reactions, enzyme-catalysed reactions display a characteristic saturation behaviour: at low substrate concentrations [S], the rate increases nearly linearly with [S], but at high [S] the rate asymptotes to a maximum velocity Vmax, no matter how much more substrate is added. This is because enzymes have a fixed number of active sites, and when all are occupied, the enzyme is working as fast as it can.
\[v = \frac{V_{\max}[\text{S}]}{K_m + [\text{S}]}\]
The Michaelis-Menten mechanism (1913) assumes a two-step process: the enzyme E reversibly binds substrate S to form the enzyme-substrate complex ES (with dissociation constant-like parameter Km), which then breaks down to release product P and regenerate E. The Michaelis constant Km is the substrate concentration at which the reaction rate is exactly half of Vmax. A small Km means the enzyme has high affinity for its substrate — the active site is half-saturated at low [S]. A large Km means the enzyme has weak affinity and needs a higher substrate concentration to operate efficiently. Km values span roughly 10 μM to 100 mM across different enzymes; they are fundamental parameters for understanding enzyme specificity.
Clinically, Km is crucial in pharmacology. Many drugs work by competitive inhibition: the drug molecule resembles the natural substrate and competes for the active site. A competitive inhibitor increases the apparent Km (makes the enzyme seem to have lower affinity) but does not change Vmax — at saturating substrate, the inhibitor is out-competed. Noncompetitive inhibitors bind a different site (the allosteric site), decreasing Vmax without affecting Km — the enzyme's affinity is unchanged, but its maximum throughput is reduced. HIV protease inhibitors, statins (cholesterol-lowering drugs), and ACE inhibitors (for hypertension) all work by exploiting Km and Vmax relationships.
The Lineweaver-Burk double-reciprocal plot (1/v vs 1/[S]) transforms the hyperbola into a straight line with y-intercept 1/Vmax and x-intercept −1/Km. Though imprecise for high-[S] data due to error amplification, it allows graphical determination of both kinetic parameters and is still widely used to classify inhibitor types by comparing the effect on the slope and intercepts.
