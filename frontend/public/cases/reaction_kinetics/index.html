Click 0th, 1st, or 2nd to switch reaction order. Drag k to change the rate constant and [A]₀ to change the initial concentration.

Watch how the curve shape changes between linear (0th), exponential (1st), and hyperbolic (2nd) decay — and observe how the t₁/₂ readout responds.

Reaction order determines the geometry of decay: zeroth order depletes at a fixed rate regardless of concentration, first order gives each molecule a constant probability of reacting per unit time (constant half-life), and second order slows dramatically as concentration falls. The half-life's dependence on [A]₀ is the sharpest experimental test — plot it at several starting concentrations and the order reveals itself. From here, the question becomes what controls the rate constant itself.

Determining order from linearization:
Plot [A] vs t → if linear, n=0. Plot ln[A] vs t → if linear, n=1. Plot 1/[A] vs t → if linear, n=2. The slope of the linear plot gives −k (n=0), −k (n=1), or +k (n=2). This is how chemists determine rate constants from experimental data.

Drag Eₐ to raise or lower the activation energy barrier. Drag T to move along the curve and observe both the cyan dot (current T) and yellow dot (T + 10 K).

Watch the k(T+10)/k(T) ratio in the readout — this is the fold-change from a 10-degree temperature increase.

The rate constant grows exponentially with temperature because only molecules in the high-energy tail of the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution clear the activation barrier. High Eₐ means that tail is thin and temperature sensitivity is extreme; enzymes evolved low Eₐ values (~20–40 kJ/mol) precisely to reduce this sensitivity near 37 °C. Catalysts accelerate reactions not by adding energy but by providing an alternative pathway with a lower barrier — the same Arrhenius exponential, smaller exponent. This sets the stage for enzyme kinetics, where saturation imposes a ceiling the Arrhenius picture alone cannot predict.

Catalysis and Eₐ: A platinum catalyst reduces the Eₐ of H₂ + O₂ → H₂O from ~240 kJ/mol to ~50 kJ/mol, making the reaction proceed at room temperature instead of requiring an ignition source. Biological enzymes are even more dramatic — they reduce Eₐ by stabilising the transition state through precise geometric and electrostatic interactions in the active site. The Arrhenius factor improvement can be 10⁸ to 10²⁰ fold compared to uncatalysed reactions.

Drag Vmax to set the enzyme's maximum throughput and Km to change substrate affinity. Move the [S] probe slider to read off the reaction rate at any substrate concentration.

Watch v/Vmax approach 1 only asymptotically — and confirm that placing the probe at [S] = Km always gives exactly 50% of Vmax.

Saturation is the hallmark of catalysis by a finite resource: once every active site is occupied, adding more substrate changes nothing. Km is the substrate concentration at half-saturation — a low Km enzyme captures substrate efficiently even at trace levels, a high Km enzyme requires abundance. Competitive inhibitors raise the apparent Km (the curve shifts right) without touching Vmax; noncompetitive inhibitors lower Vmax without shifting Km. Recognizing which parameter a drug changes tells you its mechanism without ever resolving the binding site.

Lineweaver–Burk plot diagnostic:
Competitive inhibitor: steeper slope (apparent Km increases), same y-intercept (same Vmax).
Noncompetitive inhibitor: higher y-intercept (Vmax decreases), same x-intercept (same Km).
Mixed inhibitor: both slope and intercept change. This graphical signature allows pharmacologists to determine mechanism of inhibition from kinetic data alone, without knowing the inhibitor binding site.
