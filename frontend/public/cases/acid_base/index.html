Acid–Base Chemistry

pH, buffers, and the art of proton transfer. From the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation to titration curves and buffer capacity — explore the quantitative foundations that keep blood at pH 7.4 and laboratories functioning precisely.

01 — Henderson–Hasselbalch Equation

Buffer Regions and the pH Scale

A buffer solution resists changes in pH upon addition of small amounts of acid or base. It achieves this by containing both a weak acid (HA) and its conjugate base (A⁻), which can absorb or release protons as needed. The quantitative relationship between pH and the ratio of conjugate base to weak acid is given by the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation — one of the most useful equations in all of analytical and biochemical science.
\[\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log_{10}\left(\frac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}\right)\]
When [A⁻] = [HA], the log term vanishes and pH = pKa exactly. This is the midpoint of the buffer — the point of maximum buffer capacity. When [A⁻]/[HA] = 10, pH = pKa + 1; when [A⁻]/[HA] = 0.1, pH = pKa − 1. The effective buffer range is typically defined as pKa ± 1 pH unit, the region where the buffer can meaningfully resist pH change. Outside this range, one of the two forms is so predominant that adding acid or base quickly overwhelms the system.
The biological importance of buffer systems cannot be overstated. Human blood is maintained at pH 7.35–7.45 by the bicarbonate buffer system: CO₂(aq) + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺, with an effective pKa of 6.1. At first this seems too low to buffer at pH 7.4 — that's outside the ±1 range. But the body cheats: by using the lungs to continuously exhale CO₂, it effectively controls the [CO₂] term in the equation, shifting the equilibrium. This respiratory-metabolic coupling makes the bicarbonate system far more powerful than a simple chemical buffer of the same concentration. Le Chatelier's principle at the respiratory level.

02 — Acid–Base Titration

The Sigmoidal Titration Curve

A titration is a quantitative analytical technique: a solution of known concentration (the titrant) is added to a solution of unknown concentration (the analyte) until the reaction is complete. For an acid-base titration, this endpoint is the equivalence point — where the moles of acid exactly equal the moles of base. The pH at the equivalence point depends on the nature of the acid and base: for strong acid + strong base, it is precisely 7 (neutral); for weak acid + strong base, it is above 7 (the resulting conjugate base is basic); for strong acid + weak base, below 7.
The shape of the titration curve is characteristically sigmoidal (S-shaped). Far from the equivalence point, large amounts of acid or base must be added to change the pH significantly, so the curve is relatively flat. Near the equivalence point, the pH changes extremely rapidly — a tiny addition of titrant causes a dramatic jump. This steep inflection is what makes titrations useful: it provides a sharp, easily detectable signal of the equivalence point.
\[\text{Before eq. pt.:}\quad \text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]_{\text{excess}} \qquad \text{After:}\quad \text{pH} = 14 + \log_{10}[\text{OH}^-]_{\text{excess}}\]
The choice of indicator depends on the equivalence point pH. Indicators are weak acids whose conjugate acid and base forms have different colors. The transition range is roughly pKa(indicator) ± 1. Methyl orange (pKa ≈ 3.5, transition pH 3.1–4.4, red→orange→yellow) is appropriate for titrating strong bases against strong acids. Phenolphthalein (pKa ≈ 9.1, transition pH 8.2–10, colorless→pink) is used for weak acid–strong base titrations where the equivalence point is alkaline. For a strong acid–strong base titration, either indicator works since the pH jump near the equivalence point spans from about 4 to 10 — both indicator transition ranges lie within this steep region.

Why not use pH paper? pH paper (or universal indicator) changes color continuously but lacks the precision to detect the sharp equivalence point. Indicators selected to change color at the equivalence point give a binary (color change / no color change) endpoint that a burette can track to ±0.02 mL. For 50 mL of 0.1 M acid, that's ±0.04% accuracy. For most purposes, the half-equivalence point (25 mL NaOH added here) is even more informative for weak acids — at that point, [HA] = [A⁻] exactly, so pH = pKa, giving you the pKa of the acid directly from the graph.
03 — Buffer Capacity

Quantifying Buffer Strength: β vs pH

Buffer capacity β is defined as the moles of strong acid or base required to change the pH of one liter of buffer by one unit. It is the derivative of the amount of base added with respect to pH: β = dC_b/dpH. A high β means the buffer strongly resists pH change; a low β means even small additions shift the pH dramatically. Buffer capacity is not a single number but a function of pH — it peaks at the pKa and falls off on both sides.
\[\beta = 2.303 \cdot C \cdot \frac{K_a [\text{H}^+]}{(K_a + [\text{H}^+])^2}\]
This formula reveals several important features. First, β is proportional to the total buffer concentration C = [HA] + [A⁻]: doubling the buffer concentration doubles the capacity at every pH, proportionally. Second, the term Ka[H⁺]/(Ka + [H⁺])² is maximised when Ka = [H⁺], i.e., when pH = pKa — confirming that the buffer works best at its midpoint. At pH = pKa ± 1, the capacity has fallen to about 50% of maximum. At pH = pKa ± 2, it is only about 9% of maximum — essentially negligible. This is why choosing a buffer with pKa close to your target pH is so important: a 0.1 M acetate buffer (pKa 4.76) is useless at pH 7.
In addition to the conjugate acid/base term, there are water contributions to buffer capacity at extreme pH values: β_water = 2.303([H⁺] + [OH⁻]). At pH 1, [H⁺] = 0.1 M, which is a substantial contribution; at pH 7, [H⁺] = 10⁻⁷ M and [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁷ M, which are negligible. This is why strongly acidic or alkaline solutions (pH < 2 or pH > 12) have high buffer capacity even without a weak acid/base pair.
Practical buffer selection in biochemistry requires matching pKa to target pH, choosing adequate concentration (typically 25–100 mM for intracellular conditions), and considering whether the buffer is compatible with the biological system. Common biochemical buffers include HEPES (pKa 7.55), MOPS (pKa 7.2), Tris (pKa 8.1), and MES (pKa 6.1). The so-called Good buffers were designed in 1966 to have pKa values in the physiologically relevant range of 6–8.

Why CO₂/HCO₃⁻ works as a blood buffer despite pKa = 6.1:
Normal blood pH is 7.4 — that's 1.3 units above pKa, where simple buffer capacity would be low. The trick: the body couples chemistry to physiology. If pH drops (acidosis), you breathe faster, exhaling CO₂ and shifting the equilibrium toward consuming H⁺. If pH rises (alkalosis), you breathe slower, retaining CO₂. The lungs act as a continuous concentration modulator, making the effective buffer range extend far beyond ±1 pH unit. This is Le Chatelier's principle operating at the whole-body level, with feedback time constants of seconds (respiratory) to hours (renal).
