Section 01

Descending the Loss Landscape

At the heart of every learning algorithm lies an optimization problem: find the model parameters that minimize a loss function. In deep learning, this loss function — measuring the gap between predictions and ground truth — lives in a high-dimensional space. For a network with millions of parameters, it forms a vast, rugged landscape of peaks, valleys, saddle points, and plateaus.

The gradient $\nabla L(\mathbf{w})$ points in the direction of steepest ascent — the direction that most rapidly increases the loss. To minimize loss, we move in the opposite direction: we subtract the gradient. This is the fundamental intuition of gradient descent. At each step, we measure the local slope of the loss surface and take a small step downhill.

The size of that step is controlled by the learning rate $\eta$. Choose too large a value and you overshoot — bouncing past minima or even diverging entirely. Choose too small a value and convergence becomes glacially slow, especially in the early stages when gradients are large. The ideal learning rate itself changes throughout training, motivating the scheduling strategies we explore in Section 3.

Non-convex loss landscapes (the norm in deep learning) introduce another crucial factor: initialization. Where you start determines which minimum you reach. Strategies like Xavier initialization (scaling weights by $\sqrt{1/n_{\text{in}}}$, preserving variance through layers) and He initialization (scaling by $\sqrt{2/n_{\text{in}}}$, correcting for ReLU activations) are carefully designed to start gradient descent in a regime where it can make rapid progress.

$$\mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t - \eta \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} L(\mathbf{w}_t)$$

Section 02

Comparing Optimizers

Plain gradient descent struggles on loss surfaces with different curvatures along different directions — a common situation in deep networks where some parameters are highly sensitive and others are nearly flat. On an elongated bowl, where the gradient is steep in one direction and shallow in another, vanilla SGD zigzags furiously while making slow progress along the valley floor.

Momentum addresses this by accumulating a velocity vector: instead of taking a step purely based on the current gradient, we maintain a running average of past gradients. The parameter $\beta = 0.9$ means the velocity is a mix of 10% current gradient and 90% accumulated momentum. This has a beautiful physics analogy: a ball rolling down a hill carries kinetic energy that helps it coast through flat regions and dampens the oscillations caused by steep transverse gradients.

Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation) goes further by maintaining per-parameter adaptive learning rates. It tracks both the first moment (mean of gradients, like momentum) and the second moment (uncentered variance). Parameters with consistently large gradients get smaller effective learning rates; rarely updated parameters get larger steps. The bias correction terms — dividing by $(1 - \beta_1^t)$ and $(1 - \beta_2^t)$ — correct for the fact that the moment estimates start near zero in early training.

Modern deep learning overwhelmingly defaults to Adam, but SGD with momentum often achieves better generalization on well-tuned problems — the "sharp minimum" hypothesis suggests that adaptive methods may converge to flatter, more generalizable minima less reliably. The choice is nuanced and task-dependent.

$$\text{Adam: } \hat{m}_t = \frac{m_t}{1-\beta_1^t},\quad \hat{v}_t = \frac{v_t}{1-\beta_2^t},\quad \mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t - \frac{\eta\, \hat{m}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_t}+\varepsilon}$$

Section 03

Learning Rate Schedules

If there is one hyperparameter that dominates all others in its impact on training, it is the learning rate. But no single fixed value works well throughout the entire course of training. Early on, large gradients and uninformed parameters mean a moderate learning rate makes rapid progress. Later, as the optimizer approaches a minimum, the same learning rate causes destructive oscillations around the solution.

Step decay — halving or reducing the learning rate by a factor of 10 at fixed epoch milestones — was the original engineering heuristic, still widely used in computer vision. But it requires manual tuning of the decay schedule. Cosine annealing provides a smooth, principled alternative: the learning rate follows a half-cosine from $\eta_{\max}$ down to $\eta_{\min}$, allowing gradual deceleration without abrupt transitions that can cause loss spikes.

Warmup schedules address a subtle problem in transformer training: in the first iterations, the model's parameters are random, and the Adam moment estimates are unreliable (the bias correction is large). A large initial learning rate can cause catastrophic divergence. Warmup linearly increases the learning rate from near-zero to the target value over the first few hundred or thousand steps, allowing moment estimates to stabilize before the optimizer makes large updates.

The 1cycle policy (popularized by fast.ai and Leslie Smith's research) takes this further: first increasing the learning rate to a peak, then annealing it down, and finishing with a brief phase at very low LR. This aggressive schedule acts as a form of implicit regularization — the brief high-LR phase pushes the model out of sharp minima toward wider, more generalizable basins.

$$\eta_{\text{cosine}}(t) = \eta_{\min} + \frac{\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min}}{2}\left(1 + \cos\!\left(\frac{t}{T}\pi\right)\right)$$
