A Study in Rational Conflict

The Prisoner's Dilemma

Game Theory  ·  Nash Equilibria  ·  Evolutionary Dynamics

Section I

The Dilemma

Two suspects are arrested and held in separate cells, unable to communicate. The prosecutor lacks sufficient evidence for a conviction on the principal charge and offers each prisoner an identical deal: betray your partner (Defect) or remain silent (Cooperate). The tragedy of the situation is that the individually rational choice leads both players to a collectively inferior outcome — the very essence of what makes this problem so enduringly profound.

The payoff matrix captures the structure precisely. If both cooperate, each serves a short sentence: \((-1, -1)\). If one defects while the other cooperates, the defector goes free \((0)\) while the cooperator receives the maximum sentence \((-5)\). If both defect, both receive a moderately harsh sentence \((-3, -3)\). Notice that Defect strictly dominates Cooperate: regardless of what the other player does, you always fare better by defecting. This asymmetry between individual rationality and collective welfare is the dilemma.

A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where no player can improve their payoff by unilaterally changing strategy. Here, (Defect, Defect) is the unique Nash equilibrium — neither player has an incentive to deviate. Yet the social optimum — the outcome maximizing total welfare — is (Cooperate, Cooperate) with a combined payoff of \(-2\) versus the Nash equilibrium's combined \(-6\). This gap between individual rationality and collective optimality is not merely an academic curiosity; it models arms races, climate negotiations, corporate price wars, and countless social dilemmas.

Formal Nash equilibrium: a profile \((s_1^*, s_2^*)\) such that for all players \(i\):

\[ u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*) \quad \forall\, s_i \in S_i \]

Dominance of Defection. For Player A, whatever Player B plays:

\[ u_A(D, C) = 0 > -1 = u_A(C, C) \quad\text{and}\quad u_A(D, D) = -3 > -5 = u_A(C, D) \]

So \(D\) strictly dominates \(C\). By symmetry, the same holds for Player B. Hence \((D,D)\) is the unique dominant-strategy Nash equilibrium.

Section II

Iterated Games & Cooperation

The one-shot analysis is damning: rational actors must defect. But the world is full of repeated interactions. When the same players meet again and again, the future casts a shadow backward, making cooperation potentially sustainable. Robert Axelrod's landmark 1980 computer tournament invited game theorists to submit strategies for an iterated Prisoner's Dilemma played over 200 rounds. The tournament winner — and winner of the sequel — was the disarmingly simple strategy Tit-for-Tat (TfT).

TfT has four elegant properties. It is nice: it never defects first. It is retaliatory: it immediately punishes defection in the next round. It is forgiving: once the opponent cooperates again, TfT returns to cooperation. And it is clear: its behavior is so transparent that sophisticated opponents quickly learn what to expect. These properties stand in sharp contrast to always-defect (AD), which may score points in early rounds but fails to exploit the potential for mutual cooperation. Grim Trigger is the other extreme: cooperate until the opponent defects once, then defect forever — maximally punishing but unforgiving.

The mathematics of the repeated game introduces a discount factor \(\delta \in [0,1)\) representing how much players value future payoffs relative to present ones. When \(\delta\) is sufficiently large — players care enough about the future — mutual cooperation can be sustained as a Nash equilibrium. The Folk Theorem formalizes this: any payoff that strictly dominates the minimax payoff can be sustained as a Nash equilibrium in the infinitely repeated game, provided \(\delta\) is large enough. In the finite case, backward induction unravels cooperation, but in practice, uncertain endpoints and reputation effects restore it.

Total discounted payoff over \(N\) rounds with discount factor \(\delta\):

\[ V = \sum_{t=0}^{N-1} \delta^t \cdot r_t \]

TfT vs Always Defect over \(N\) rounds (undiscounted, starting TfT cooperates round 0):

\[ V_{\text{TfT}} = -5 + (-3)(N-1), \quad V_{\text{AD}} = 0 + (-3)(N-1) \]

AD always scores 5 more than TfT when playing against TfT. But TfT vs TfT scores \(-1 \cdot N\), while AD vs AD scores \(-3 \cdot N\). The collective payoff under mutual TfT dominates mutual AD for all \(N \geq 2\).

Section III

Evolutionary Dynamics

Beyond rational deliberation lies a deeper question: which strategies survive and spread in a population of agents playing repeatedly against one another? Evolutionary game theory, pioneered by John Maynard Smith, answers this without assuming that players consciously optimize. Instead, strategies that earn above-average payoffs are imitated and replicated; those that underperform shrink. The mathematical vehicle is the replicator equation.

In a population with three strategy types — Always Cooperate (AC), Always Defect (AD), and Tit-for-Tat (TfT) — each strategy's fitness depends on its expected payoff against the current population mix. When the population is mostly cooperators, AD invades easily: defectors exploit cooperators and multiply. But a critical mass of TfT players can resist this invasion: TfT punishes defectors immediately while still cooperating with fellow TfT players. This creates a kind of conditional cooperation that is both exploitable only at a cost and mutually rewarding.

The dynamics are frequency-dependent: the fitness of a strategy depends not on any intrinsic value, but on who else is in the population. This leads to rich behavior: bistability (where the final state depends sensitively on initial conditions), limit cycles, and even chaos in larger strategy spaces. The fundamental lesson is that cooperation is not a given — it must be maintained by the right structural conditions, whether through iteration, reputation, kin selection, or network structure. The replicator dynamics reveal when those conditions are met.

Replicator equation for strategy \(i\) with population fraction \(x_i\):

\[ \dot{x}_i = x_i \bigl( f_i - \bar{f} \bigr) \]

where the fitness of strategy \(i\) against the current population is:

\[ f_i = \sum_j x_j \cdot \pi(i, j) \]

and the mean population fitness is \(\bar{f} = \sum_i x_i f_i\). Strategies with \(f_i > \bar{f}\) grow; those with \(f_i < \bar{f}\) shrink. Fixed points occur at \(x_i = 0\) or \(f_i = \bar{f}\).
