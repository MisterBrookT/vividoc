Information Theory · Shannon-Hartley

Channel Capacity

The maximum rate at which information can be reliably transmitted over a noisy channel — a mathematical limit as fundamental as the speed of light, governing everything from satellite links to 5G.

01 — Binary Symmetric Channel

When Bits Flip: The BSC

The simplest noisy channel is the Binary Symmetric Channel (BSC). A sender transmits a single bit; with probability \(\varepsilon\) (the crossover probability) the bit flips in transit; with probability \(1-\varepsilon\) it arrives intact. The channel is "symmetric" because both 0 and 1 are equally likely to be corrupted. Despite its simplicity, the BSC captures the essential tension between noise and communication.
Shannon's 1948 theorem defines channel capacity \(C\) as the maximum mutual information over all possible input distributions: \(C = \max_{p(X)} I(X;Y)\). For the BSC, this maximum is achieved with a uniform input (\(P(X=0)=0.5\)), giving \(C = 1 - H_b(\varepsilon)\) bits per channel use, where \(H_b(\varepsilon) = -\varepsilon\log_2\varepsilon - (1-\varepsilon)\log_2(1-\varepsilon)\) is the binary entropy function.
The result has a beautiful interpretation. At \(\varepsilon=0\), the channel is perfect: every bit gets through, capacity is 1 bit per use. At \(\varepsilon=0.5\), the channel is pure coin-flip noise: the output is completely independent of the input, capacity is 0. Between these extremes, capacity falls as a smooth concave curve — symmetric around \(\varepsilon=0.5\).
Shannon's channel coding theorem is arguably the most important result in communication theory. It says: for any rate \(R < C\) and any \(\varepsilon > 0\), there exists a code with block length \(n\) (possibly large) achieving error probability below \(\varepsilon\). Conversely, no code with rate \(R > C\) can achieve arbitrarily low error probability. The capacity \(C\) is an exact threshold — there is no "almost reliable" communication above capacity.
In practice, USB 2.0 achieves error rates around \(10^{-12}\) per bit using simple CRC codes — the channel's physical error rate is so low (far below \(\varepsilon = 0.5\)) that almost no coding is needed. Deep-space links (Voyager, Mars rovers) operate near capacity because every watt of transmit power is precious.

Reliable communication above capacity is impossible: This is the converse of Shannon's theorem. If you try to transmit at rate \(R > C\), the error probability cannot be driven below some positive constant, no matter how sophisticated the code. This is not a practical limitation but a mathematical theorem — it follows from the data processing inequality and the chain rule for mutual information.
02 — AWGN Channel and the Shannon-Hartley Theorem

Bandwidth, Power, and the Limits of Wireless

Real wireless channels don't flip discrete bits — they add continuous Gaussian noise to analog signals. The Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN) channel model captures this: the received signal is \(Y = X + Z\) where \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, N_0/2)\) per dimension. The Shannon-Hartley theorem gives the capacity of this channel exactly: \(C = B \log_2(1 + \text{SNR})\) bits per second, where \(B\) is bandwidth in Hz and SNR is the signal-to-noise ratio.
This formula has profound engineering consequences. Doubling bandwidth doubles capacity (linear scaling). But doubling SNR (equivalently, doubling transmit power) only adds \(B\) bits/s — a logarithmic gain. At high SNR, power has diminishing returns: going from 30 dB to 40 dB SNR gains the same capacity as 20 dB to 30 dB. This is why spectral efficiency (bits/s/Hz) is the primary design metric in 5G, not raw power.
As \(B \to \infty\) with fixed total power \(P\), the noise power also grows (more bandwidth admits more noise). The capacity does not diverge; it approaches a finite limit \(C \to P/(N_0 \ln 2)\). This "wideband limit" means you can't communicate infinitely fast just by spreading over unlimited bandwidth — you need SNR, not just bandwidth.
Wi-Fi 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6) achieves up to 9.6 Gbps using 160 MHz channels with 1024-QAM modulation at SNR around 35 dB — operating at perhaps 70–80% of Shannon capacity. 5G NR uses up to 400 MHz channels in the mmWave band (28 GHz). Modern fiber optics use wavelength-division multiplexing to achieve >100 Tbps on a single fiber, each wavelength operating near its Shannon limit.

Spectral efficiency: The ratio C/B = log₂(1 + SNR) bits/s/Hz is the key metric. Shannon capacity sets the upper bound; practical systems fall short due to implementation losses, channel estimation errors, and hardware nonlinearity. 5G NR targets 30 bits/s/Hz in ideal conditions — requiring SNR > 10⁹ in linear scale (i.e., ~90 dB), only achievable in very short-range mmWave links with highly directional antennas.
03 — Approaching the Shannon Limit

From Theory to Practice: 70 Years of Coding

Shannon proved in 1948 that capacity-achieving codes exist. He did not say how to build them. For the next 45 years, coding theorists searched for practical codes that could approach the Shannon limit. Simple codes like Hamming (1950) and BCH (1960) offered elegant algebraic structure but operated far from capacity. The gap to Shannon was measured in decibels — a monument to the distance between existence and construction.
The breakthrough came in 1993. Claude Berrou and colleagues at France Télécom invented Turbo codes — a concatenated code with iterative "turbo" decoding that could operate within 0.5 dB of capacity. The coding community was stunned. Shortly after, LDPC codes (invented by Gallager in 1962 but forgotten) were rediscovered by Richardson and Urbanke (2001), who showed LDPC codes could asymptotically achieve capacity on any memoryless channel.
The metric of choice is bit error rate (BER) vs \(E_b/N_0\) — energy per bit over noise spectral density. The Shannon limit for reliable binary communication is \(E_b/N_0 = \ln 2 \approx -1.59\) dB (in the infinite-bandwidth limit). Uncoded systems require ~8–10 dB to achieve BER of \(10^{-5}\). Turbo and LDPC codes can achieve the same BER at 0–1 dB — a gain of ~8 dB, corresponding to 6× less transmit power.
Today, LDPC codes are used in Wi-Fi (802.11n/ac/ax), 5G NR, DVB-S2 satellite broadcasting, and 10G Ethernet. Polar codes (Arıkan, 2009) — the first provably capacity-achieving code with efficient encoding and decoding — are used in the 5G control channel. Deep-space missions use concatenated Reed-Solomon + convolutional codes (Voyager) or Turbo codes (Mars Science Laboratory).
The lesson for information theory and machine learning alike: the existence of an optimal solution (proven by Shannon, or by a convergence theorem in ML) does not tell you how to find it. The constructive problem — building the optimal code, or training the optimal model — often takes decades. But the theoretical limit serves as an invaluable target and sanity check.

Polar codes and the end of the quest: In 2009, Erdal Arıkan proved that polar codes achieve the Shannon capacity of any binary-input memoryless symmetric channel with encoding and decoding complexity \(O(n \log n)\). This was the first constructive proof that capacity is achievable with polynomial-complexity coding. 3GPP chose polar codes for the 5G control channel (PBCH, PDCCH) in 2016 — the first major standard to use a capacity-achieving code by construction, not just by approximation.
